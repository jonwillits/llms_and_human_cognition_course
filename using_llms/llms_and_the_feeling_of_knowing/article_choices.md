## How to Find and Download Your Paper

1. Go to [Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com).
2. Search for the paper's title (e.g., *"Reasoning about a Rule"*) or paste the full reference.
3. Click the title to go to the publisher page, or look for a **[PDF]** link on the right side.
4. If the PDF is not freely available, try clicking **"All versions"** beneath the result — sometimes an open-access copy is hosted on a university or author website.
5. You can also paste the DOI (e.g., `https://doi.org/10.1080/14640746808400161`) directly into your browser address bar to reach the publisher page.
6. If you still cannot access the paper, use the University of Illinois library proxy: go to the [library website](https://www.library.illinois.edu), search for the article title or DOI, and download through the library's access.

## Topic A — Human Reasoning and Decision Making

### Wason (1968) — "Reasoning about a Rule"
Wason, P. C. (1968). Reasoning about a rule. *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology*, 20(3), 273–281. https://doi.org/10.1080/14640746808400161

Wason presented participants with four cards, each showing a number on one side and a letter on the other. Participants had to decide which cards to turn over to test a conditional rule (e.g., "If a card has a vowel on one side, it has an even number on the other"). Most participants systematically failed — they chose cards that could confirm the rule but neglected the card that could falsify it.

*Application question*: A university introduces a new policy: "If a student receives a failing grade in a class required for their major, their advisor must schedule a meeting." You are auditing compliance and have four student records: (A) a student who received a failing grade, (B) a student who did not, (C) a student whose advisor scheduled a meeting, (D) a student whose advisor did not schedule a meeting. Which records must you check to test whether the policy is being followed, and why do people tend to get this type of problem wrong? What are some details from the study that might apply in this situation, and what are some details that might not transfer to this new situation?

### Lord, Ross, & Lepper (1979) — "Biased Assimilation and Attitude Polarization"
Lord, C. G., Ross, L., & Lepper, M. R. (1979). Biased assimilation and attitude polarization: The effects of prior theories on subsequently considered evidence. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 37(11), 2098–2109. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.37.11.2098

Participants who held strong prior opinions on capital punishment (for or against) read a mixed set of research studies — some supporting and some undermining the death penalty's effectiveness. Rather than moving toward the evidence, both groups rated the studies supporting their own position as more convincing and became *more* extreme in their original beliefs after reading the same materials.

*Application question*: Two colleagues disagree about whether remote work improves or hurts productivity. They both read the same research report containing mixed evidence. Based on this study's findings, what would you predict happens to each person's beliefs after reading the report, and what does this reveal about how prior beliefs shape evidence evaluation? What are some details from the study that might apply in this situation, and what are some details that might not transfer to this new situation?

### Frederick (2005) — "Cognitive Reflection and Decision Making"
Frederick, S. (2005). Cognitive reflection and decision making. *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 19(4), 25–42. https://doi.org/10.1257/089533005775196732

Frederick introduced the Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT) — three problems that each have an intuitive but wrong answer and a correct answer that requires overriding that initial impulse (e.g., "A bat and a ball cost $1.10 in total. The bat costs $1.00 more than the ball. How much does the ball cost?"). CRT performance predicted a wide range of decision-making behaviors, including time preferences, risk preferences, and susceptibility to cognitive biases — often more strongly than standard intelligence or numeracy measures.

*Application question*: A friend says she is a great critical thinker because she did well in a formal logic class in college. But she routinely falls for misleading product comparisons and anchoring effects when shopping. Based on this paper, how would you explain this discrepancy, and what does the CRT measure that a logic test might not? What are some details from the study that might apply in this situation, and what are some details that might not transfer to this new situation?

## Topic B — Human Concept and Category Learning

### Posner & Keele (1968) — "On the Genesis of Abstract Ideas"
Posner, M. I., & Keele, S. W. (1968). On the genesis of abstract ideas. *Journal of Experimental Psychology*, 77(3), 353–363. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0025953

Participants studied random dot patterns that were distortions of an unseen prototype pattern. At test, they classified new patterns — including the never-seen prototype and novel distortions. The key finding was that participants classified the prototype more accurately than many of the patterns they had actually studied, suggesting they had abstracted a central tendency from the training examples.

*Application question*: A medical student has reviewed many X-rays showing a particular type of fracture during her training. When shown a "textbook-perfect" example she has never actually seen before, she identifies it faster and more confidently than some of the actual training cases she studied. How would this paper's findings explain her experience? What are some details from the study that might apply in this situation, and what are some details that might not transfer to this new situation?

### Collins & Quillian (1969) — "Retrieval Time from Semantic Memory"
Collins, A. M., & Quillian, M. R. (1969). Retrieval time from semantic memory. *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*, 8(2), 240–247. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(69)80069-1

Participants verified sentences like "A canary is a bird" or "A canary is an animal" as quickly as possible. Verification time increased with the hierarchical distance between the subject and the category — "A canary is an animal" took longer than "A canary is a bird." Property verification followed the same pattern: "A canary can sing" (stored at the canary level) was faster than "A canary can fly" (stored at the bird level). The results supported a hierarchical network model in which properties are stored at the highest applicable level to avoid redundancy.

*Application question*: It takes you longer to verify "A canary is an animal" than "A canary is a bird," but verifying "A canary can sing" is faster than verifying "A canary can fly." Explain why, according to this paper's model. Then identify one type of sentence or category that this model would have trouble explaining. What are some details from the study that might apply in this situation, and what are some details that might not transfer to this new situation?

### Dopkins & Gleason (1997) — "Comparing Exemplar and Prototype Models of Categorization"
Dopkins, S., & Gleason, T. (1997). Comparing exemplar and prototype models of categorization. *Canadian Journal of Experimental Psychology*, 51(3), 212–230. https://doi.org/10.1037/1196-1961.51.3.212

This study tested predictions of prototype models (which assume people classify items by comparing them to an abstracted category average) against exemplar models (which assume people compare new items to stored individual examples). By constructing stimulus spaces where the two models make diverging predictions, the authors were able to directly test which account better described participants' categorization behavior.

*Application question*: Two cognitive scientists disagree about how people categorize music into genres. One argues that people compare new songs to an "average" or "ideal" example of each genre. The other argues that people compare new songs to specific songs they remember. Based on this paper's approach, what kind of experiment could help distinguish between these two accounts, and what pattern of results would favor each model? What are some details from the study that might apply in this situation, and what are some details that might not transfer to this new situation?

## Topic C — Human Memory

### Roediger & McDermott (1995) — "Creating False Memories: Remembering Words Not Presented in Lists"
Roediger, H. L., & McDermott, K. B. (1995). Creating false memories: Remembering words not presented in lists. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 21(4), 803–814. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.21.4.803

Participants studied lists of words that were all associated with a single non-presented "critical lure" (e.g., they heard *bed, rest, awake, tired, dream* but never *sleep*). On both recall and recognition tests, participants frequently and confidently "remembered" the critical lure — even claiming to remember the specific moment it was presented. The rate of false recall for the lure was comparable to recall rates for actually presented words.

*Application question*: An eyewitness to a car accident confidently "remembers" that the car ran a red light, even though the traffic signal was never visible in any footage. Other witnesses described the scene using language that strongly implied a red-light violation, but no one actually stated it. Based on this paper, how would you explain how someone can have a confident, detailed memory of something that never happened? What are some details from the study that might apply in this situation, and what are some details that might not transfer to this new situation?

### Bransford & Johnson (1972) — "Contextual Prerequisites for Understanding"
Bransford, J. D., & Johnson, M. K. (1972). Contextual prerequisites for understanding: Some investigations of comprehension and recall. *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*, 11(6), 717–726. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(72)80006-9

Participants read an ambiguous passage that was nearly incomprehensible without context (the most famous example describes the process of doing laundry in abstract, procedural language without ever naming the activity). Participants who were given the topic *before* reading recalled significantly more than those given it *after* reading or not at all. The study demonstrated that comprehension — having a meaningful framework to organize incoming information — is a prerequisite for memory, not just a helpful addition.

*Application question*: A student reads a dense passage about an unfamiliar scientific procedure. She reads it carefully, twice, but remembers almost nothing afterward. A classmate then tells her what the passage is about, and on her next read she finds it easy to understand and remember. Based on this paper, why did a simple contextual label make such a dramatic difference, and what does this reveal about the relationship between comprehension and memory? What are some details from the study that might apply in this situation, and what are some details that might not transfer to this new situation?

### Morris, Bransford, & Franks (1977) — "Levels of Processing versus Transfer-Appropriate Processing"
Morris, C. D., Bransford, J. D., & Franks, J. J. (1977). Levels of processing versus transfer appropriate processing. *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*, 16(5), 519–533. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(77)80016-9

This paper challenged the levels-of-processing framework by showing that "deep" (semantic) encoding does not always produce better memory than "shallow" (phonemic) encoding. When tested with a standard recognition test, semantic encoding was superior. But when tested with a rhyme recognition test — which required recognizing words that rhymed with studied words — phonemic encoding produced better performance. The key insight is that the effectiveness of an encoding strategy depends on the match between how information is encoded and how it is tested.

*Application question*: A student studies vocabulary words by focusing on their meanings (semantic processing) and does well on a standard vocabulary test. But when tested on which of those words rhymed with words from a separate list, she performs poorly — worse than a classmate who studied by focusing on how the words sounded. How does this paper explain why "deeper" processing doesn't always win, and what principle should guide how you study for different kinds of tests? What are some details from the study that might apply in this situation, and what are some details that might not transfer to this new situation?
