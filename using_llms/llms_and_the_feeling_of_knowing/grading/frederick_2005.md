# Article Content — Frederick (2005)
## "Cognitive Reflection and Decision Making"

Frederick, S. (2005). Cognitive reflection and decision making. *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 19(4), 25–42.

### Application Question

A friend says she is a great critical thinker because she did well in a formal logic class in college. But she routinely falls for misleading product comparisons and anchoring effects when shopping. Based on this paper, how would you explain this discrepancy, and what does the CRT measure that a logic test might not? What are some details from the study that might apply in this situation, and what are some details that might not transfer to this new situation?

---

## Model Answers and Scoring Criteria

### Question 1: Key Finding

**Model answer:** The CRT is a three-item test in which each problem has an intuitive but incorrect answer and a correct answer that requires overriding that impulse. People who score higher on the CRT — meaning they more consistently override their intuitive responses — show more normatively rational decision-making across a wide range of tasks, including time preferences (choosing larger later rewards over smaller sooner ones), risk preferences, and resistance to classic cognitive biases such as framing effects and conjunction errors. This relationship holds even after controlling for other measures of cognitive ability such as SAT scores and numeracy.

**Score 2** if the answer conveys both: (a) the CRT measures the tendency to override an intuitive but wrong response, AND (b) higher CRT scores predict more rational decision-making behavior across multiple domains.

**Score 1** if the answer captures only one of those two elements — for example, correctly describes what the CRT measures but does not mention its predictive relationship to decision-making, or mentions prediction without explaining what the test actually measures.

**Score 0** if the answer is vague, incorrect, or blank — for example, describes the CRT only as a measure of intelligence or math skill without the key idea of overriding intuition.

### Question 2: Method

**Model answer:** Frederick administered the three CRT problems to large samples, primarily college students across multiple universities. Participants answered the three problems, generating a CRT score from 0 to 3. The same participants also completed decision-making tasks — such as choosing between smaller-sooner and larger-later monetary amounts, or between sure gains and gambles — allowing CRT scores to be correlated with decision outcomes. In several studies participants also completed other cognitive ability measures (e.g., SAT scores, numeracy scales), enabling Frederick to test whether the CRT predicted decision-making above and beyond general ability.

**Score 2** if the answer conveys both: (a) participants completed the three CRT problems to produce a score, AND (b) those scores were correlated with decision-making task outcomes. The answer should reflect that this is a correlational study, not an experiment with a manipulation.

**Score 1** if the answer describes the CRT administration accurately but omits the decision-making correlation component, or describes some decision-making element but mischaracterizes the CRT portion.

**Score 0** if the answer is vague, blank, or fundamentally wrong — for example, describes the study as a training experiment or confuses it with a different study design.

### Question 3: Broader Concept

**Model answer:** This paper illustrates dual-process cognition — the idea that human reasoning operates through two systems: a fast, automatic, intuitive mode (System 1) that produces quick responses with little effort, and a slower, deliberate, reflective mode (System 2) that can override those intuitive responses when engaged. The CRT is specifically designed to measure whether a person tends to engage this reflective process to catch and correct initial intuitions. This distinction matters because many real-world judgment errors — susceptibility to framing, anchoring, and other biases — arise not from ignorance but from failing to engage reflective reasoning in contexts that do not obviously demand it.

**Score 2** if the answer identifies the dual-process framework (System 1/System 2, fast/slow, intuitive/reflective, or equivalent language) AND explains why it matters in terms of real-world judgment or decision-making errors. The exact terms "System 1/System 2" are not required, but the two-mode structure must be present.

**Score 1** if the answer gestures at the right idea but is imprecise — for example, says "people make mistakes when they don't think carefully" without the dual-process framing, or names the framework without explaining its significance.

**Score 0** if the answer misidentifies the concept or is too vague to demonstrate understanding — for example, describes the paper as being about intelligence differences or memory.

### Question 4: Apply

**Model answer:** Formal logic tests measure performance when the task is explicitly framed as a reasoning problem requiring careful attention — conditions that naturally prompt System 2 engagement. The CRT measures something different: whether a person spontaneously overrides an initial intuitive response in a context that does not announce the need for effortful reasoning. Shopping environments are designed to trigger fast, intuitive responses, and Frederick's data suggest that CRT performance — not logic test performance — predicts susceptibility to this kind of bias. This directly explains the friend's pattern.

What transfers: The finding that CRT scores predict real-world decision biases (anchoring, framing) in naturalistic contexts maps well to the shopping scenario. The friend's pattern of strong explicit-reasoning performance with weak spontaneous-reflection is precisely what the CRT measures.

What may not transfer: Frederick's studies were correlational and conducted in controlled settings — they do not show that practicing CRT-style reflection would reduce real-world bias. The shopping context also involves real money, time pressure, and emotional states not present in the CRT setting. Susceptibility to specific marketing tactics may also partly reflect unfamiliarity with those tactics rather than a stable cognitive style.

**Score 2** if the answer (a) correctly distinguishes formal logic performance (prompted, explicit reasoning) from CRT performance (spontaneous override of intuition) using the paper's framework, AND (b) identifies at least one meaningful limitation or non-transferable detail.

**Score 1** if the answer addresses the discrepancy reasonably but either does not connect to the CRT's specific mechanism, or does not identify any limitation in applying the finding to this scenario.

**Score 0** if the answer does not engage with the paper's framework, or is vague and generic — for example, "she probably just isn't paying attention when shopping."
