# Article Content — Wason (1968)
## "Reasoning about a Rule"

Wason, P. C. (1968). Reasoning about a rule. *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology*, 20(3), 273–281.

### Application Question

A university introduces a new policy: "If a student receives a failing grade in a class required for their major, their advisor must schedule a meeting." You are auditing compliance and have four student records: (A) a student who received a failing grade, (B) a student who did not, (C) a student whose advisor scheduled a meeting, (D) a student whose advisor did not schedule a meeting. Which records must you check to test whether the policy is being followed, and why do people tend to get this type of problem wrong? What are some details from the study that might apply in this situation, and what are some details that might not transfer to this new situation?

---

## Model Answers and Scoring Criteria

### Question 1: Key Finding

**Model answer:** When given four cards and asked which to turn over to test a conditional rule (e.g., "If there is a vowel on one side, there is an even number on the other"), most participants chose the confirming cards — the vowel card and the even-number card — and neglected the card that could falsify the rule (the odd-number card). Very few participants selected the logically correct pair (the vowel card and the odd-number card). This pattern demonstrates confirmation bias: people seek evidence that supports a hypothesis rather than evidence that could disconfirm it, even when disconfirmation is the logically appropriate strategy.

**Score 2** if the answer conveys both: (a) participants systematically failed the selection task by choosing confirming rather than falsifying cards, AND (b) this demonstrates a bias toward confirmation over falsification of hypotheses.

**Score 1** if the answer captures only one of those elements — for example, correctly describes that participants chose wrong cards but does not connect this to the confirmation/falsification distinction, or mentions confirmation bias without accurately describing the card selection pattern.

**Score 0** if the answer is vague, incorrect, or blank — for example, describes the result only as "people are bad at logic" without the specific pattern of choosing confirming rather than falsifying cards.

### Question 2: Method

**Model answer:** Participants were presented with four cards, each showing a letter on one side and a number on the other. They were told a conditional rule — typically "If there is a vowel on one side of the card, then there is an even number on the other side" — and asked which cards they needed to turn over to determine whether the rule was being followed. The four cards showed, for example, a vowel, a consonant, an even number, and an odd number. The dependent measure was which cards participants chose to turn over. The logically correct answer is the vowel card (which could falsify the rule if it has an odd number on the back) and the odd-number card (which could falsify the rule if it has a vowel on the back).

**Score 2** if the answer conveys both: (a) participants were shown cards with a letter/number rule and asked which to turn over, AND (b) the study measured which cards participants selected (i.e., this is an observational/response-recording study, not a training or intervention experiment).

**Score 1** if the answer describes the card-and-rule setup but omits what was measured, or describes the measurement correctly but mischaracterizes the task.

**Score 0** if the answer is vague, blank, or fundamentally wrong — for example, describes the study as an experiment about memory or describes it as involving verbal arguments rather than card-selection tasks.

### Question 3: Broader Concept

**Model answer:** This paper illustrates confirmation bias — the tendency to seek evidence that confirms a hypothesis rather than evidence that could falsify it. More precisely, in the context of formal logic, it illustrates that human reasoning often fails to apply the modus tollens operation: if "P implies Q" is the rule, then finding "not-Q" would falsify it, but people rarely seek such falsifying cases. This bias has wide consequences beyond card puzzles: it shapes how people evaluate scientific evidence, maintain beliefs in the face of counterevidence, and assess claims in everyday reasoning. The Wason task became a central paradigm for studying deductive reasoning and the conditions under which it breaks down.

**Score 2** if the answer identifies both: (a) the confirmation bias principle (seeking confirming rather than falsifying evidence), AND (b) why this matters — either with reference to real-world reasoning, belief maintenance, or the broader failure of normative logical inference.

**Score 1** if the answer names confirmation bias or the logic failure without explaining its significance, or describes the broader significance in terms too vague to demonstrate understanding of the specific mechanism.

**Score 0** if the answer misidentifies the concept (e.g., calls it a memory bias or an attention failure) or is too vague to show understanding.

### Question 4: Apply

**Model answer:** The correct records to check are A and D. Record A (failing grade) must be checked to see whether a meeting was scheduled — if no meeting was scheduled, the policy is violated. Record D (no meeting scheduled) must be checked to see whether the student had a failing grade — if they did, the policy is violated. Record B (no failing grade) is irrelevant because the rule says nothing about what advisors must do when students pass. Record C (meeting scheduled) is also irrelevant because the rule does not prevent advisors from scheduling meetings for other reasons.

People tend to get this wrong for the same reason as in the Wason task: they check A and C (the affirming cases — failing grade and meeting scheduled) rather than A and D (the case that could violate and the case that could reveal a violation). They look for examples that match the rule rather than examples that would break it.

What transfers: The logical structure is identical to the Wason task — a conditional rule, four cases, and the same tendency to check the antecedent and consequent rather than the antecedent and the negated consequent. The confirmation bias pattern should apply directly.

What may not transfer: The university scenario involves real-world stakes (compliance monitoring, academic outcomes), which may increase motivation to reason carefully. The "realistic" cover story might actually improve performance — research has shown that logically equivalent problems framed as social rule compliance are solved more accurately than abstract card versions. Familiarity with bureaucratic auditing logic could also help.

**Score 2** if the answer (a) correctly identifies A and D as the records to check (or explains the equivalent logic without using the labels), AND (b) identifies at least one meaningful reason why the analogous problem is hard or at least one real limit on how well the card-task findings transfer to this setting.

**Score 1** if the answer identifies the right records but does not engage with why people err or with transfer limitations, or engages with the difficulty/transfer question but identifies incorrect records.

**Score 0** if the answer identifies incorrect records without any correct reasoning, or is too vague to demonstrate engagement with the paper's framework.
