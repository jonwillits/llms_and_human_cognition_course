# Article Content — Morris, Bransford, & Franks (1977)
## "Levels of Processing versus Transfer-Appropriate Processing"

Morris, C. D., Bransford, J. D., & Franks, J. J. (1977). Levels of processing versus transfer appropriate processing. *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*, 16(5), 519–533.

### Application Question

A student studies vocabulary words by focusing on their meanings (semantic processing) and does well on a standard vocabulary test. But when tested on which of those words rhymed with words from a separate list, she performs poorly — worse than a classmate who studied by focusing on how the words sounded. How does this paper explain why "deeper" processing doesn't always win, and what principle should guide how you study for different kinds of tests? What are some details from the study that might apply in this situation, and what are some details that might not transfer to this new situation?

---

## Model Answers and Scoring Criteria

### Question 1: Key Finding

**Model answer:** Semantic (deep) encoding did not always produce better memory than phonemic (shallow) encoding. On a standard recognition test, semantically encoded words were recognized better — replicating the standard levels-of-processing advantage. But when participants were given a rhyme recognition test (recognizing words that rhymed with studied words), phonemic encoding produced better performance than semantic encoding. The pattern showed that memory performance depends not on depth of encoding alone, but on the match between how information is encoded and how it is subsequently tested — a principle the authors called transfer-appropriate processing.

**Score 2** if the answer conveys both: (a) shallow phonemic encoding outperformed deep semantic encoding on the rhyme test (a reversal of the standard levels-of-processing advantage), AND (b) this demonstrates that encoding effectiveness depends on the match between encoding type and test type.

**Score 1** if the answer correctly describes the reversal (phonemic beats semantic on the rhyme test) without explicitly articulating the transfer-appropriate processing principle, or correctly states the transfer-appropriate processing principle without describing the specific crossover pattern that demonstrated it.

**Score 0** if the answer is vague, incorrect, or blank — for example, says only "different study strategies work for different tests" without the specific finding about semantic vs. phonemic encoding and the rhyme test reversal.

### Question 2: Method

**Model answer:** Participants performed orienting tasks while studying a list of words. In the semantic encoding condition, they answered questions about meaning — for example, deciding whether a word fit into a meaningful sentence context. In the phonemic encoding condition, they answered questions about sound — for example, deciding whether a word rhymed with a target word. After studying all words, participants were assigned to one of two test conditions: a standard recognition test (identify words you saw) or a rhyme recognition test (identify words that rhymed with words you saw). The 2×2 design — encoding type (semantic vs. phonemic) crossed with test type (standard vs. rhyme recognition) — allowed the researchers to directly assess whether encoding-test match, rather than encoding depth alone, determined memory performance.

**Score 2** if the answer conveys both: (a) participants encoded words under either semantic or phonemic orienting tasks, AND (b) the test was either standard recognition or rhyme recognition — and the key design feature is the 2×2 cross of encoding type by test type.

**Score 1** if the answer correctly describes the encoding manipulation but omits the two test conditions or does not indicate that the critical comparison was the interaction between encoding type and test type.

**Score 0** if the answer is vague, blank, or fundamentally wrong — for example, describes a single test condition only, or describes this as a study-strategy training experiment rather than an orienting task design.

### Question 3: Broader Concept

**Model answer:** This paper illustrates transfer-appropriate processing — the principle that the effectiveness of an encoding strategy is determined not by its "depth" in an absolute sense, but by how well the processing at encoding overlaps with the processing required at retrieval. Craik and Lockhart's levels-of-processing framework had claimed that deeper (semantic) processing always produced better memory. Morris et al. showed that this advantage reverses when the memory test requires the kind of processing done during the shallow encoding condition. The principle has practical implications for studying and learning: rereading a text (a form of semantic processing) may produce good performance on recall tests but poor performance on tests requiring recognition of surface features. More generally, it means that good encoding strategies are test-specific, not universally superior.

**Score 2** if the answer identifies both: (a) transfer-appropriate processing — encoding effectiveness depends on the match between encoding and retrieval conditions, AND (b) why this matters — either by explaining how it challenges or qualifies the levels-of-processing framework, or by noting its implications for how people should study or how memory should be tested.

**Score 1** if the answer correctly identifies transfer-appropriate processing without explaining how it challenges the levels-of-processing account, or correctly contrasts levels-of-processing vs. transfer-appropriate processing without clearly stating the transfer principle.

**Score 0** if the answer misidentifies the concept (e.g., describes it as a study about forgetting or encoding variability without the transfer-appropriate processing principle) or is too vague to demonstrate understanding.

### Question 4: Apply

**Model answer:** According to Morris et al., the student's semantic study strategy was well-matched to the standard vocabulary test — both involved processing meaning. But the rhyme test required phonemic information (how words sound), which was not encoded by the semantic strategy. Her classmate's phonemic strategy, though "shallower" by the levels-of-processing account, was transfer-appropriate for the rhyme test: the processing done at encoding directly matched the processing required at retrieval.

The principle this illustrates is that effective studying is not about studying "harder" or "deeper" in the abstract, but about studying in a way that matches the cognitive demands of the test. If a test requires recognizing rhymes, phonological encoding during study is appropriate. If a test requires understanding and applying concepts, semantic encoding is appropriate.

What transfers: The scenario is nearly identical to the Morris et al. design — two types of encoding (semantic/meaning-focused vs. phonemic/sound-focused) and two types of tests (standard vs. rhyme-based). The crossover pattern should apply directly.

What may not transfer: The original study used simple word lists and orienting tasks in a laboratory. Real vocabulary learning involves richer encoding (context sentences, usage examples, visual presentation) that goes beyond a single semantic or phonemic dimension. In real learning, most encoding is mixed rather than purely semantic or phonemic, making the transfer-appropriate principle harder to apply cleanly. Additionally, vocabulary tests in a course may have different demands than a controlled recognition test in a lab — essay or application-based tests require production and use, not just recognition, which may further complicate which encoding strategy is optimal.

**Score 2** if the answer (a) explains the student's performance using transfer-appropriate processing — semantic encoding did not overlap with the phonemic demands of the rhyme test, AND (b) identifies at least one meaningful reason why the principle may be less straightforwardly applicable in real learning contexts.

**Score 1** if the answer correctly invokes transfer-appropriate processing but does not engage with transfer limitations, or engages thoughtfully with limitations but mischaracterizes why the semantic studier performed poorly (e.g., attributes it to insufficient effort rather than encoding-test mismatch).

**Score 0** if the answer does not invoke the transfer-appropriate processing principle, or explains the outcome only in terms of study time or effort rather than encoding-test match.
