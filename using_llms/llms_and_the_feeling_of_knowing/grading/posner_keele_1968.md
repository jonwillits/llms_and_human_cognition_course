# Article Content — Posner & Keele (1968)
## "On the Genesis of Abstract Ideas"

Posner, M. I., & Keele, S. W. (1968). On the genesis of abstract ideas. *Journal of Experimental Psychology*, 77(3), 353–363.

### Application Question

A medical student has reviewed many X-rays showing a particular type of fracture during her training. When shown a "textbook-perfect" example she has never actually seen before, she identifies it faster and more confidently than some of the actual training cases she studied. How would this paper's findings explain her experience? What are some details from the study that might apply in this situation, and what are some details that might not transfer to this new situation?

---

## Model Answers and Scoring Criteria

### Question 1: Key Finding

**Model answer:** Participants who studied distorted versions of random dot patterns — but never saw the underlying prototype from which those distortions were generated — could nonetheless classify the unseen prototype as accurately as, or more accurately than, many of the distorted patterns they had actually studied. This result shows that participants had abstracted a central tendency from their training examples: they formed a mental representation of the prototype pattern even though it was never presented. The finding provides direct evidence for prototype abstraction — the idea that category learning involves constructing an internal average or summary rather than merely storing individual examples.

**Score 2** if the answer conveys both: (a) participants classified the never-seen prototype well — as well as or better than some training items, AND (b) this demonstrates that learners abstracted a central tendency (prototype) from their experience with distorted examples.

**Score 1** if the answer captures only one element — for example, correctly describes the prototype-superiority result but does not connect it to the process of abstraction, or correctly mentions prototype abstraction without describing the specific performance pattern that demonstrated it.

**Score 0** if the answer is vague, incorrect, or blank — for example, describes the study as showing that "people are good at recognizing patterns" without the specific finding about the unseen prototype's performance advantage.

### Question 2: Method

**Model answer:** Random dot patterns were generated as distortions of hidden prototype patterns — the prototype itself was never shown. Participants studied a set of these distorted training patterns. At test, they were shown new patterns including: the never-previously-seen prototype, the same distortions they had studied, and new distortions at varying degrees of distance from the prototype. Participants categorized each test item, and their classification accuracy (and sometimes reaction time) was the main dependent variable. The critical comparison was between performance on the unseen prototype versus performance on previously studied training items.

**Score 2** if the answer conveys both: (a) participants studied distorted versions of an unseen prototype pattern, AND (b) the test included the novel prototype and accuracy was compared across studied items and the new prototype.

**Score 1** if the answer describes the training phase correctly but omits the critical test comparison (prototype vs. training items), or describes the test correctly but mischaracterizes training (e.g., says participants studied the prototype directly).

**Score 0** if the answer is vague, blank, or fundamentally wrong — for example, describes this as a word or object study rather than dot patterns, or confuses it with a reaction-time or priming study without the categorization task.

### Question 3: Broader Concept

**Model answer:** This paper illustrates prototype theory of concept learning — the idea that when people learn a category, they form a mental representation of the central tendency or "average" of the category members, not just a list of individual examples they have encountered. This prototype then serves as a reference point for classifying new items: items close to the prototype are categorized easily and accurately, while items far from it are categorized less well. The finding has broad implications for how humans represent categories of all kinds — visual patterns, objects, social groups, concepts — and stands in contrast to exemplar theory, which proposes that classification is based on similarity to individual stored examples rather than an abstracted prototype.

**Score 2** if the answer identifies both: (a) the prototype theory claim — learners abstract a central tendency from examples rather than just storing instances, AND (b) why this matters or what it challenges — either contrasting it with exemplar theory, explaining how prototypes guide categorization, or noting the broader implications for category representation.

**Score 1** if the answer correctly identifies prototype abstraction but does not explain its broader significance, or gestures at the right idea (e.g., "people form general impressions") without clearly describing the prototype mechanism.

**Score 0** if the answer misidentifies the concept (e.g., describes it as a perceptual memory study or an attention study) or is too vague to demonstrate understanding.

### Question 4: Apply

**Model answer:** According to this paper, the medical student has abstracted a prototype — a mental representation of the central tendency of fractures from all the training X-rays she has studied. The textbook-perfect example is, by definition, close to or identical to that abstracted prototype. Because it matches her internalized average so well, she classifies it faster and more confidently than some of the idiosyncratic training cases, which were distortions from the prototype at various distances. This is essentially the Posner & Keele result: the unseen prototype is classified as well as or better than the studied examples.

What transfers: The core mechanism maps well — learning through exposure to variable examples leads to prototype abstraction, and the prototype can be recognized even when it was never directly encountered. The superiority of the central-tendency item over some training items is exactly what the paper predicts.

What may not transfer: Posner & Keele used arbitrary random dot patterns with no prior meaning. X-rays are meaningful medical images where the student brings substantial existing knowledge and conceptual structure. The prototype abstraction in the paper was driven purely by perceptual averaging, while the medical student's recognition likely involves both perceptual and semantic/conceptual processing. Expertise and formal training may also produce more structured or multi-level representations than the relatively simple prototype abstraction demonstrated with dot patterns. Additionally, individual fractures vary in ways that may not be captured by a single prototype — there may be subtypes requiring multiple prototypes.

**Score 2** if the answer (a) explains the medical student's advantage by connecting it to prototype abstraction — that the textbook-perfect example matches her internally abstracted central tendency, AND (b) identifies at least one meaningful way the original findings may not fully transfer to this medical expertise context.

**Score 1** if the answer correctly applies the prototype abstraction explanation but does not engage with transfer limitations, or engages thoughtfully with transfer limits but mischaracterizes the prototype mechanism.

**Score 0** if the answer does not invoke the prototype abstraction concept, or is too vague to demonstrate engagement with the paper's specific finding.
