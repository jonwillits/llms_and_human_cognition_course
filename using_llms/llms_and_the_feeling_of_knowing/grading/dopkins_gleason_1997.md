# Article Content — Dopkins & Gleason (1997)
## "Comparing Exemplar and Prototype Models of Categorization"

Dopkins, S., & Gleason, T. (1997). Comparing exemplar and prototype models of categorization. *Canadian Journal of Experimental Psychology*, 51(3), 212–230.

### Application Question

Two cognitive scientists disagree about how people categorize music into genres. One argues that people compare new songs to an "average" or "ideal" example of each genre. The other argues that people compare new songs to specific songs they remember. Based on this paper's approach, what kind of experiment could help distinguish between these two accounts, and what pattern of results would favor each model? What are some details from the study that might apply in this situation, and what are some details that might not transfer to this new situation?

---

## Model Answers and Scoring Criteria

### Question 1: Key Finding

**Model answer:** By designing stimulus spaces in which prototype and exemplar models predicted different categorization boundaries, the study was able to directly pit the two models against each other. The results provided evidence favoring the exemplar model over the prototype model in key conditions: participants' categorization behavior was better predicted by similarity to stored individual examples than by similarity to an abstracted category average. The key methodological contribution was the use of stimulus spaces engineered to produce diverging model predictions, enabling a decisive comparison rather than relying on conditions where both models fit the data.

**Score 2** if the answer conveys both: (a) the study found evidence favoring the exemplar model (or found that exemplar predictions better fit the data in the critical conditions), AND (b) this was achieved by designing stimuli where the two models made opposing predictions.

**Score 1** if the answer correctly describes the diverging-prediction methodology without clearly stating which model was supported, or correctly states that exemplar models were favored without explaining how the experiment was able to distinguish between the two accounts.

**Score 0** if the answer is vague, incorrect, or blank — for example, says only "people categorize things in complex ways" or confuses which model was supported, or does not indicate that a comparison between models was the point.

### Question 2: Method

**Model answer:** Participants categorized geometric stimuli (such as dot patterns or shapes varying on multiple dimensions) that were designed with a specific structure: the stimulus space was arranged so that the prototype model and the exemplar model predicted different category boundaries for certain test items. Participants first learned to categorize training stimuli with feedback, then were tested on new items — including items in regions of the stimulus space where the two models' predictions diverged. Their categorization responses were compared to the predictions of both models to determine which model better accounted for their behavior. The study used a model-fitting approach in which the fit of each model to participant data was formally evaluated.

**Score 2** if the answer conveys both: (a) participants categorized stimuli after a learning phase, AND (b) the key feature of the design was that stimuli were constructed so that the two models made different predictions, allowing a formal model comparison.

**Score 1** if the answer describes a categorization learning task but omits the critical feature that stimuli were designed to produce diverging predictions, or mentions the diverging-prediction design without describing the learning-and-test procedure.

**Score 0** if the answer is vague, blank, or fundamentally wrong — for example, describes a verbal or semantic categorization task, or describes the study as a reaction-time experiment without the learning and model-comparison components.

### Question 3: Broader Concept

**Model answer:** This paper illustrates the prototype versus exemplar debate in category learning theory — two competing accounts of how humans represent and use categories. Prototype models propose that people abstract a summary representation (the central tendency or ideal) from category members and classify new items by comparing them to that abstracted prototype. Exemplar models propose that people store individual category members in memory and classify new items by comparing them to all stored examples. The key methodological insight the paper demonstrates is how to design studies that can actually distinguish between these two accounts: by finding conditions where the models make different predictions and testing those conditions empirically, rather than relying on cases where both models fit equally well.

**Score 2** if the answer identifies both: (a) the prototype vs. exemplar distinction — what each model claims about category representation, AND (b) the broader methodological principle — that good theory tests require finding conditions where competing theories make different predictions.

**Score 1** if the answer correctly contrasts prototype and exemplar models without articulating the methodological contribution, or correctly describes the diverging-prediction methodology without clearly explaining what the two models claim.

**Score 0** if the answer misidentifies the concept (e.g., conflates this with prototype theory alone, or describes it as a study about memory for individual items rather than models of categorization) or is too vague to demonstrate understanding.

### Question 4: Apply

**Model answer:** To distinguish the prototype and exemplar accounts of genre categorization, an experiment would need to identify songs that the two models predict differently. A useful approach: construct a set of training songs that define a genre, then create test songs in regions of musical space where the prototype account (similarity to the average song) and the exemplar account (similarity to individual training songs) make different predictions. For example, a song that is similar to one particular memorable training song but distant from the genre average would be classified as a genre member under the exemplar model but not the prototype model; a song equidistant from several training songs but close to the genre average would be classified as a member under the prototype model. Participants would categorize these test songs, and the pattern of responses in the critical regions would indicate which model better predicted their behavior.

What transfers: The core methodological strategy — find stimulus items where the models make different predictions, test those items, and compare model fits to behavior — is directly applicable. Music can be represented in multidimensional feature spaces just as the geometric stimuli were.

What may not transfer: Music genres are learned through years of real-world exposure with enormous variability and personal history, not through a brief controlled learning phase in a laboratory. Genre membership is also culturally negotiated and fuzzy in ways that go beyond a binary classification task. Additionally, musical features are far more complex and correlated than the simple geometric dimensions in the study, making it harder to construct the carefully controlled stimulus spaces that made the model comparison decisive.

**Score 2** if the answer (a) describes an experimental design that correctly applies the diverging-prediction logic — identifying test items where prototype and exemplar models predict differently and seeing which pattern emerges, AND (b) identifies at least one meaningful reason why this approach may be harder to apply or less decisive in the music domain.

**Score 1** if the answer describes a reasonable categorization experiment but does not specifically apply the diverging-prediction methodology, or correctly describes the methodology without identifying any transfer limitations.

**Score 0** if the answer describes an experiment that cannot distinguish the two models (e.g., simply asking people to categorize songs without the critical diverging-prediction manipulation), or does not engage with the paper's methodological approach.
