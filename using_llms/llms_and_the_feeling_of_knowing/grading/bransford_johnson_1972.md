# Article Content — Bransford & Johnson (1972)
## "Contextual Prerequisites for Understanding"

Bransford, J. D., & Johnson, M. K. (1972). Contextual prerequisites for understanding: Some investigations of comprehension and recall. *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*, 11(6), 717–726.

### Application Question

A student reads a dense passage about an unfamiliar scientific procedure. She reads it carefully, twice, but remembers almost nothing afterward. A classmate then tells her what the passage is about, and on her next read she finds it easy to understand and remember. Based on this paper, why did a simple contextual label make such a dramatic difference, and what does this reveal about the relationship between comprehension and memory? What are some details from the study that might apply in this situation, and what are some details that might not transfer to this new situation?

---

## Model Answers and Scoring Criteria

### Question 1: Key Finding

**Model answer:** Participants who were given the topic of an ambiguous passage *before* reading it recalled significantly more information than participants who received the topic *after* reading or not at all — even though all groups read the same passage. In a key experiment, participants who received no topic (or who received the topic after reading) rated the passage as incomprehensible and recalled very little. Participants who received the topic before reading found the passage comprehensible and recalled substantially more. This demonstrated that a meaningful organizational framework — provided in advance — is a prerequisite for comprehension and memory, not merely a helpful supplement.

**Score 2** if the answer conveys both: (a) receiving the topic before reading produced better recall than receiving it after (or not at all), AND (b) the key interpretation is that prior context/schema is *necessary* for comprehension — not just helpful — because without it the passage cannot be meaningfully organized.

**Score 1** if the answer correctly describes the before/after recall difference without explaining *why* prior context matters, or correctly identifies schema/context as necessary for comprehension without describing the experimental pattern that demonstrated it.

**Score 0** if the answer is vague, incorrect, or blank — for example, says only "knowing what you're reading about helps you remember it" without the specific before-vs.-after design or the prerequisite interpretation.

### Question 2: Method

**Model answer:** Participants were randomly assigned to conditions: (1) topic given before reading, (2) topic given after reading, (3) no topic given, or (4) in some experiments, a partial context was provided. All participants read the same ambiguous passage — most famously, a passage describing the abstract steps of doing laundry without naming the activity ("The procedure is actually quite simple. First you arrange things into different groups..."). After reading, participants rated how comprehensible they found the passage and then recalled as many ideas from the passage as they could. The key dependent variables were comprehension ratings and number of idea units recalled.

**Score 2** if the answer conveys both: (a) participants read the same ambiguous passage under different context conditions (before, after, or no topic), AND (b) recall and/or comprehension ratings were the dependent variables.

**Score 1** if the answer describes the context manipulation but omits what was measured, or describes recall as the measure without explaining that the same ambiguous passage was read across all conditions.

**Score 0** if the answer is vague, blank, or fundamentally wrong — for example, describes participants as reading different passages, or describes a study where they are taught a topic and then tested on unrelated material.

### Question 3: Broader Concept

**Model answer:** This paper illustrates the role of schemas in comprehension and memory — the idea that understanding incoming information requires an existing mental framework (schema) into which the information can be organized. Without a relevant schema, information cannot be meaningfully integrated and therefore cannot be encoded into memory effectively. The finding extends to all learning contexts: lectures, textbooks, scientific papers, and technical documents that presuppose background knowledge are harder to understand and remember not because they are written poorly, but because the reader lacks the schema needed to make sense of the material. This connects to broader constructivist theories of learning, which hold that knowledge is not passively received but actively constructed on existing foundations.

**Score 2** if the answer identifies both: (a) schemas (or background knowledge, mental frameworks, prior context) are necessary for comprehension and memory — not just helpful, AND (b) why this matters broadly — either by noting implications for instruction, learning difficult material, or the constructive nature of comprehension.

**Score 1** if the answer correctly identifies schemas as important without conveying the prerequisite-not-just-helpful distinction, or notes the practical implications without clearly connecting them to the schema/comprehension mechanism.

**Score 0** if the answer misidentifies the concept (e.g., describes it as a study about attention or motivation) or is too vague to demonstrate understanding.

### Question 4: Apply

**Model answer:** According to Bransford & Johnson, the student's difficulty was not a failure of attention or effort — she read the passage twice carefully. The problem was that without knowing the topic, she had no schema to organize the incoming information. Each sentence could be parsed at a surface level but could not be integrated into a coherent meaning structure. When her classmate provided the topic label, she suddenly had a framework that made the passage cohere: she could now map each procedural step onto a familiar structure, allowing meaningful encoding and therefore memory. This illustrates the paper's central claim: comprehension — organizing information into a meaningful structure — is a prerequisite for memory, not just a useful aid.

What transfers: The scenario closely mirrors the Bransford & Johnson design — a student struggling with an ambiguous or unfamiliar passage who gains dramatically from a contextual label provided in advance of a second reading. The mechanism (schema allows meaningful encoding) is directly applicable.

What may not transfer: In the Bransford & Johnson study, the passages were deliberately constructed to be maximally ambiguous without context — the "doing laundry" passage made essentially no sense without the topic. A dense scientific procedure passage, by contrast, may be comprehensible in parts even without a global label; the difficulty may partly reflect unfamiliar vocabulary or conceptual complexity, not pure schema absence. Also, the original experiment used a controlled between-subjects design (different participants got different conditions), while the student here read the passage twice — potential fatigue or boredom effects could interact with the schema effect. Additionally, the original study was about recall of idea units, while a science course context may require deeper procedural understanding beyond recall.

**Score 2** if the answer (a) explains the improvement by invoking the schema/comprehension prerequisite mechanism — the label provided an organizing framework that made meaningful encoding possible, AND (b) identifies at least one meaningful way this scenario differs from the original study (e.g., the passage may not be purely ambiguous, vocabulary is a separate issue, the repeated reading adds complexity).

**Score 1** if the answer correctly invokes the schema mechanism but does not engage with transfer limitations, or engages thoughtfully with limitations but attributes the improvement to an unrelated factor (e.g., simple familiarity or motivation) without mentioning schemas.

**Score 0** if the answer does not invoke the schema/comprehension mechanism, or is too vague to demonstrate engagement with the paper's central claim.
