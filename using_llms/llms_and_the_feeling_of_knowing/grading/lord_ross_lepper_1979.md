# Article Content — Lord, Ross, & Lepper (1979)
## "Biased Assimilation and Attitude Polarization"

Lord, C. G., Ross, L., & Lepper, M. R. (1979). Biased assimilation and attitude polarization: The effects of prior theories on subsequently considered evidence. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 37(11), 2098–2109.

### Application Question

Two colleagues disagree about whether remote work improves or hurts productivity. They both read the same research report containing mixed evidence. Based on this study's findings, what would you predict happens to each person's beliefs after reading the report, and what does this reveal about how prior beliefs shape evidence evaluation? What are some details from the study that might apply in this situation, and what are some details that might not transfer to this new situation?

---

## Model Answers and Scoring Criteria

### Question 1: Key Finding

**Model answer:** Participants who held strong prior views on capital punishment — both those who supported it and those who opposed it — read the same mixed set of research studies containing evidence both for and against the deterrence effect of the death penalty. Rather than updating their beliefs toward the evidence, both groups rated the studies supporting their own position as more credible and methodologically sound, while dismissing the opposing studies as flawed. The result was that both groups became *more* extreme in their original beliefs after reading the same mixed evidence — a phenomenon the authors called biased assimilation and attitude polarization.

**Score 2** if the answer conveys both: (a) participants evaluated mixed evidence in a biased way — rating pro-attitudinal studies as better quality and counter-attitudinal studies as worse, AND (b) exposure to the same mixed evidence caused attitudes to become *more* extreme rather than converging toward the evidence.

**Score 1** if the answer captures only one element — for example, correctly notes that people rated evidence in a biased way but does not mention attitude polarization, or correctly notes polarization without explaining the biased evaluation mechanism.

**Score 0** if the answer is vague, incorrect, or blank — for example, says only "people believe what they want to believe" without the specific pattern of differential quality ratings and polarization.

### Question 2: Method

**Model answer:** Participants were pre-selected for strong prior attitudes on capital punishment — half were proponents and half were opponents, screened by attitude surveys. All participants then read two research summaries: one study that had found evidence for the deterrence effect of capital punishment, and one that had found evidence against it. After reading each study, participants rated its quality and persuasiveness. Finally, they reported their attitudes again. The design was correlational and within-subjects — all participants read the same materials; the key variable was their initial attitude, and the key measures were study quality ratings and post-reading attitude change.

**Score 2** if the answer conveys both: (a) participants with opposing prior attitudes all read the same mixed evidence (pro- and anti-capital punishment studies), AND (b) the key measures included ratings of study quality/persuasiveness and post-reading attitude change.

**Score 1** if the answer describes the mixed-evidence design but omits the quality-rating measure, or describes the attitude change outcome without explaining how the mixed-evidence exposure was set up.

**Score 0** if the answer is vague, blank, or fundamentally wrong — for example, describes participants as reading different evidence, or confuses it with a debate or persuasion study.

### Question 3: Broader Concept

**Model answer:** This paper illustrates biased assimilation and motivated reasoning — the tendency to evaluate evidence through the lens of one's prior beliefs, applying more critical scrutiny to evidence that challenges existing views than to evidence that supports them. It is also a demonstration of belief perseverance: beliefs can persist — and even intensify — in the face of disconfirming evidence, because that evidence is discounted rather than integrated. The implications extend beyond attitudes about capital punishment to any domain where people hold strong prior beliefs: scientific controversies, political debates, and interpersonal conflicts. The finding challenges the assumption that exposure to balanced information is sufficient to reduce polarization.

**Score 2** if the answer identifies both: (a) the biased assimilation mechanism (prior beliefs distort evaluation of new evidence), AND (b) why this matters — either by naming the attitude polarization outcome, the belief perseverance phenomenon, or by explaining implications for real-world situations involving contested evidence.

**Score 1** if the answer correctly names confirmation bias or biased reasoning without explaining the polarization outcome, or correctly describes polarization without clearly connecting it to the biased evaluation mechanism.

**Score 0** if the answer misidentifies the concept (e.g., describes it as a memory effect or social pressure effect) or is too vague to demonstrate understanding.

### Question 4: Apply

**Model answer:** Based on this paper's findings, both colleagues would likely become more extreme in their original positions, not more moderate, after reading the same mixed report. The colleague who believes remote work helps productivity would rate the supporting evidence as more rigorous and compelling, while dismissing the opposing evidence as methodologically flawed or contextually limited. The colleague who believes remote work hurts productivity would do the reverse. Neither would converge toward the evidence.

What transfers: The logical structure maps well — two people with opposing priors evaluating the same mixed evidence. The finding that both groups will cherry-pick in favor of their priors is directly applicable. The mechanism (biased quality evaluation leading to polarization) should operate the same way.

What may not transfer: The original study used a politically charged, morally loaded topic (capital punishment). Attitudes about remote work may be less deeply held or identity-linked, potentially reducing the magnitude of biased assimilation. Additionally, participants in remote work debates may have direct personal experience (they've worked remotely), whereas the original study involved abstract policy beliefs — personal experience could either strengthen or complicate the bias. The original participants were specifically pre-selected for strong prior attitudes; colleagues in a real workplace may have more moderate or uncertain positions.

**Score 2** if the answer (a) predicts attitude polarization (not convergence) for both colleagues based on the paper's mechanism, AND (b) identifies at least one meaningful reason the original findings might not fully transfer to this new context.

**Score 1** if the answer correctly predicts polarization but does not engage with transfer limitations, or engages thoughtfully with transfer but mischaracterizes the predicted outcome.

**Score 0** if the answer predicts convergence or moderation of beliefs (the opposite of what the paper found), or is too vague to demonstrate engagement with the paper's specific mechanism.
