# Article Content — Roediger & McDermott (1995)
## "Creating False Memories: Remembering Words Not Presented in Lists"

Roediger, H. L., & McDermott, K. B. (1995). Creating false memories: Remembering words not presented in lists. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 21(4), 803–814.

### Application Question

An eyewitness to a car accident confidently "remembers" that the car ran a red light, even though the traffic signal was never visible in any footage. Other witnesses described the scene using language that strongly implied a red-light violation, but no one actually stated it. Based on this paper, how would you explain how someone can have a confident, detailed memory of something that never happened? What are some details from the study that might apply in this situation, and what are some details that might not transfer to this new situation?

---

## Model Answers and Scoring Criteria

### Question 1: Key Finding

**Model answer:** Participants who studied lists of words that were all associated with a single non-presented "critical lure" (e.g., *bed, rest, awake, tired, dream* — all associated with *sleep*, which was not in the list) frequently and confidently recalled and recognized the critical lure as if it had been presented. False recall rates for critical lures were comparable to recall rates for words that actually appeared in the lists. Participants also showed high confidence in these false memories — many reported remembering the specific moment the critical lure was presented, even though it never was. This demonstrates that memory can be systematically and reliably false even when individuals have high subjective confidence in their recollection.

**Score 2** if the answer conveys both: (a) participants frequently "remembered" the non-presented critical lure at rates comparable to actual studied words, AND (b) this occurred with high confidence — participants were not just guessing but believed they remembered the lure being presented.

**Score 1** if the answer correctly describes that false memories occurred but does not indicate the magnitude (comparable to real words) or the high confidence, or correctly notes the high confidence without describing the critical lure paradigm clearly.

**Score 0** if the answer is vague, incorrect, or blank — for example, says only "people have false memories" without the specific pattern of the critical lure or the comparable rates to real words.

### Question 2: Method

**Model answer:** Participants heard or read multiple lists of 12–15 words, each list consisting of words strongly associated with a single non-presented critical lure word. For example, participants might hear *bed, rest, awake, tired, dream, wake, snooze, blanket, doze, slumber, nap, yawn* — all associated with *sleep*, which was never presented. After each list, participants completed a free recall test. After all lists were presented, a recognition test was administered, including actually studied words, the non-presented critical lures, and unrelated control words. Participants indicated whether they recognized each word and, for some items, whether they "remembered" the specific experience of studying it. The critical dependent variables were recall and recognition rates for critical lures compared to studied words and unrelated foils.

**Score 2** if the answer conveys both: (a) participants studied word lists where all words were associates of a non-presented critical lure, AND (b) recall and/or recognition tests measured whether participants "remembered" the non-presented lure.

**Score 1** if the answer describes the associative word list structure but omits what the test measured, or describes the test (recognizing non-presented words) without clearly explaining how the lists were structured to create false memories.

**Score 0** if the answer is vague, blank, or fundamentally wrong — for example, describes a study about recognition of pictures, or a study where participants were told false information by an experimenter, or confuses this with a misinformation paradigm.

### Question 3: Broader Concept

**Model answer:** This paper illustrates constructive (or reconstructive) memory — the idea that memory is not a faithful recording system but a generative process in which the mind fills gaps with related knowledge, associations, and inferences. When people encounter a cluster of associated concepts, the underlying associative structure activates related concepts that were never directly experienced, and those activations can later be (mis)attributed to actual perceptual experience. This has profound implications for eyewitness testimony, recovered memory debates, and any situation in which high subjective confidence is treated as evidence that a memory is accurate. The DRM (Deese–Roediger–McDermott) paradigm became one of the most widely replicated false memory demonstrations in cognitive psychology.

**Score 2** if the answer identifies both: (a) memory is constructive/reconstructive — the mind fills in missing information based on associations, AND (b) why this matters — either by noting implications for eyewitness testimony, the unreliability of high confidence, or the generative/inferential nature of memory more broadly.

**Score 1** if the answer correctly identifies memory as constructive without explaining why this matters, or discusses the practical implications (e.g., false eyewitness memories) without clearly connecting them to the constructive memory mechanism.

**Score 0** if the answer misidentifies the concept (e.g., describes it as a study about forgetting or interference rather than false construction) or is too vague to demonstrate understanding.

### Question 4: Apply

**Model answer:** According to Roediger & McDermott's framework, the eyewitness's false memory arises from the same mechanism that produces false recall of critical lures: the associative context activates a concept (*ran a red light*) that was never directly experienced, and that activation is later attributed to direct perceptual experience rather than to inference or associative spread. The language used by other witnesses — implying a violation without stating it — functions like the associated word list in the DRM paradigm: it activates the critical concept, and that activation is later misattributed to a memory of actually seeing the light.

What transfers: The basic mechanism — associative activation leading to confident false memory — maps well. The high confidence in the false memory is also predicted: participants in the DRM paradigm were not guessing; they were genuinely convinced they had encountered the critical lure. The socially transmitted nature of the activation (hearing it from other witnesses) is analogous to the list-based activation in the original study.

What may not transfer: The DRM paradigm involves verbal word lists in a laboratory, while eyewitness memory involves complex visual scenes with emotional arousal, real-world stakes, and post-event social influence. The level of false memory in the lab may not predict the exact rate or severity in real legal contexts. Additionally, the DRM paradigm uses a single controlled category of associates; eyewitness memory involves multiple overlapping event schemas. Social pressure, media exposure, and leading questions add additional mechanisms beyond the pure associative spreading activation the paper studied.

**Score 2** if the answer (a) explains the eyewitness's false memory using the associative activation / constructive memory mechanism from the paper, AND (b) identifies at least one meaningful reason the lab DRM findings may not directly translate to real-world eyewitness scenarios.

**Score 1** if the answer correctly invokes the constructive memory or associative activation explanation without addressing transfer limitations, or engages thoughtfully with transfer limitations but does not clearly connect the explanation to the paper's specific mechanism.

**Score 0** if the answer does not invoke the paper's mechanism (e.g., attributes the false memory only to lying, social pressure, or general forgetfulness without the associative construction explanation), or is too vague to demonstrate engagement with the paper.
