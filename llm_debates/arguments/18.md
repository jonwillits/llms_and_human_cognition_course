A model is scientifically useful to the degree that it makes accurate, non-obvious predictions about the phenomenon it models. By that standard, LLMs are the best models of human language processing we have ever had. LLM surprisal scores — the model's uncertainty about each word — predict human reading times better than any prior model. LLM-derived semantic representations predict neural activity in language-processing brain regions. LLMs replicate human-like ambiguity resolution, structural priming, and frequency effects that took decades of psycholinguistic research to characterize. No previous cognitive model predicted all of these things simultaneously. If prediction is the standard — and for scientific models, it should be — LLMs pass.

Human brains are biological neural networks. LLMs are artificial neural networks. Both learn from exposure to language. Both encode representations distributed across many interconnected units. Both show context-sensitive generalization. These architectural and functional parallels are not superficial: they reflect deep similarities in how information is organized and processed. Because the architectures share so many properties, it would be surprising if LLMs didn't capture something important about how human brains handle language. The architectural similarity is itself evidence that the two systems are modeling the same underlying cognitive processes.

A good cognitive model does not merely predict average human behavior on benchmark tasks. It predicts the specific pattern of human successes and failures — the particular cases where humans find things easy, the particular cases where they find things hard, and the specific kinds of errors they make when they fail. This is the pattern that reveals underlying mechanisms. LLMs get this pattern systematically wrong. They fail catastrophically on spatial reasoning tasks that human toddlers handle easily. They succeed effortlessly at memorization tasks that challenge human experts. Their errors on language tasks, when they occur, do not resemble human errors. A model that predicts average task performance but misrepresents the structure of success and failure is not modeling the cognitive processes responsible for human behavior — it is tracking some surface statistical regularity that happens to correlate with average outcomes without capturing the underlying mechanism.

Human cognition is fundamentally shaped by the body. Concepts of space, time, force, and causality are acquired through sensorimotor interaction with the physical world — reaching, grasping, falling, pushing. Abstract thinking builds on these embodied foundations through metaphor and schematic extension. LLMs have no body, no sensorimotor experience, no real-world action, and no developmental trajectory of physical engagement with the environment. Therefore, whatever LLMs are doing when they process language, it cannot be a model of human cognition, which is thoroughly embodied from the ground up.
