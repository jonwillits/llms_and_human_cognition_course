Most knowledge work is a mixture of routine and non-routine tasks. A lawyer spends hours reviewing documents before spending minutes exercising legal judgment. A researcher spends days compiling literature before spending hours thinking about what it means. A doctor reads test results and chart notes before making a diagnostic decision. In each case, the non-routine component — judgment, synthesis, creative problem-solving — is the most valuable part, and it is bottlenecked by the time consumed by routine preparation. LLMs can automate or accelerate much of the routine component without touching the non-routine core. This is augmentation in the most literal sense: the human expert does what they are best at, and the tool handles the rest. The result is not fewer experts — it is more productive experts who can apply their judgment to more cases, more patients, more projects.

The history of technology and employment does not support the prediction that automation causes permanent, widespread unemployment. Every major wave of automation — agricultural mechanization, industrial machinery, computerization — eliminated specific categories of work while creating new ones that were not foreseeable in advance. Computerization destroyed the typing pool and the filing clerk; it created the web developer, the data analyst, and the UX designer. The jobs that emerged were not simply the old jobs in new clothing — they were genuinely new categories of work that required new skills, served new needs, and often provided better working conditions than the jobs they replaced. LLMs will follow the same pattern. They will eliminate some existing roles, transform others, and create new roles — prompt engineering, AI auditing, human-AI workflow design, AI-assisted research — that do not yet have well-defined descriptions. Predicting permanent net job loss from LLMs requires believing that this cycle has finally broken, and the historical record provides no strong basis for that belief.

Expertise does not appear fully formed. It develops through a long pipeline in which novices perform routine, supervised work that gradually builds the judgment, pattern recognition, and contextual understanding that characterize expert performance. Junior lawyers review documents to learn what matters in a contract. Junior doctors take histories to learn what symptoms cluster together. Junior programmers write boilerplate code to internalize the architecture of working systems. This routine work is not busywork — it is the developmental substrate on which expertise is built. When LLMs automate the routine tasks at the base of the expertise pipeline, they remove the training ground. If a junior lawyer never reviews documents because an LLM does it, they never develop the judgment that document review was building. If a junior doctor never takes a full history because an LLM pre-summarizes the chart, they never learn to notice what the summary leaves out. The result, over a generation, is a workforce that has access to powerful tools but lacks the deep understanding needed to use those tools wisely or to recognize when they fail.

Previous waves of automation unfolded over decades, allowing labor markets, educational systems, and social institutions to adapt gradually. Agricultural mechanization displaced workers over a century. Computerization transformed white-collar work over several decades. In each case, the pace of displacement was slow enough that retraining programs could develop, new industries could emerge, and displaced workers could, in many cases, transition within their working lives. LLM automation operates on a fundamentally different timescale. The capabilities that were research demonstrations in 2022 are production systems in 2025. Entire categories of knowledge work — content writing, basic legal analysis, customer support, routine coding — are being automated in years, not decades. The social infrastructure that enables adaptation — educational curricula, retraining programs, labor policy, new industry development — cannot operate at this speed. The result is a structural mismatch: the technology displaces workers faster than the systems designed to absorb displaced workers can respond. Even if the long-term equilibrium looks like previous technological transitions, the transition itself may involve unprecedented levels of concentrated, rapid displacement.
