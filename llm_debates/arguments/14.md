Fodor and Pylyshyn identified a fundamental property of thought that any adequate cognitive system must capture: systematicity. If you can think the thought "Mary admires John," you can also think "John admires Mary." These two thoughts are not independent — they share constituents and structure. Any system capable of the first must, in virtue of that very capability, be capable of the second. This is only possible if the system has internal representations that are genuinely compositional — representations with constituent structure where the pieces can be rearranged to produce new wholes. Distributed representations — including the kind LLMs use — smear information across connection weights without preserving constituent structure in the required way. A system that lacks constituent structure in its representations may produce individual outputs that resemble systematic thought without actually having the architecture that systematicity requires. This is not a matter of scale or training data; it is a structural fact about how information is encoded.

The record shows that LLMs consistently fail on tasks that require strict rule application. They make errors in formal logic that a simple rule-following system would not make. They fail on mathematical induction when problems are stated in slightly unfamiliar notation. They violate simple compositional rules when asked to combine known concepts in novel ways. Classical computers that use explicit symbolic representations do not make these kinds of errors when the symbolic rules are correctly specified. This performance gap is not random noise — it reflects something systematic about the difference between explicit symbol-based reasoning and learned pattern completion. The failures tell us that LLMs don't have symbols, and if they don't have symbols, they can't genuinely reason compositionally.

The classical argument for symbols rests on an architectural claim: only systems with explicit discrete representations can support the compositional operations that thought requires. But this argument assumes that compositionality is an all-or-nothing, architectural property. There is a different way to look at it. What compositionality actually requires is that a system generalize systematically across novel combinations of concepts — if it has learned "red" and "cube," it can handle "red cube" without additional training. LLMs demonstrably do this. They handle novel noun-adjective combinations, novel syntactic constructions, novel cross-domain analogies, and novel compositional descriptions of physical scenarios without those specific combinations appearing in training data. If the behavioral capacity for compositional generalization is what the symbols-requirement is tracking, then LLMs satisfy it. The architectural claim turns out to be stronger than the phenomenon it was supposed to explain.

The most powerful argument against the symbols-requirement is the one in front of us: LLMs are astonishingly capable. They solve problems across virtually every cognitive domain — reasoning, language, planning, creativity, knowledge synthesis. If explicit symbols were truly necessary for cognitive competence, a system without them should fail at cognitive tasks. LLMs don't fail — they often succeed at human or superhuman levels. Whatever they're using instead of explicit symbols is working. The empirical success of the technology is itself a refutation of the claim that symbols are required.
