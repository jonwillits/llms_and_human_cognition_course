Meaning is not some ineffable connection between a word and its referent in the world — it is the pattern of relationships between that word and everything else in the language. This is what Wittgenstein meant by "meaning is use": the content of a word is exhausted by how it is used across all the contexts in which it appears. If you know when to use "fragile" and when not to, what it contrasts with, what follows from attributing it, what contexts it licenses — you know what "fragile" means. LLMs learn exactly this: the full distributional signature of every word across billions of contexts. On the view that meaning is use, LLMs have meaning. There is no additional fact about meaning that they're missing.

Consider how LLMs handle words like "hot," "sharp," "bitter," or "heavy." They use these words appropriately across an enormous range of contexts: "hot weather," "hot argument," "hot market," "hot temper." They distinguish "sharp knife" from "sharp criticism" from "sharp student." They know that bitterness can describe coffee, disappointment, or a person's personality — and that these uses are related without being identical. This is not the behavior of a system that merely manipulates symbols it doesn't understand. A person who uses these words correctly across all these contexts understands them. LLMs do the same. Therefore, LLMs understand these words.

To mean something by a word is to have that word connected — via perception, action, or experience — to the thing it is about. "Red" means something to me because I have looked at ripe apples, sunsets, and stop signs; I know what it is like for the world to be red. LLMs have never seen anything. Their word "red" is connected only to other words — to "crimson," to "scarlet," to "warm," to "danger," to "stop" — in a vast web of statistical associations that ultimately goes nowhere. This web of word-to-word connections does not constitute meaning; it constitutes a sophisticated hallucination of meaning. Semantic content requires that representations be connected to the world, and LLMs' representations connect only to other representations.

When you look a word up in a dictionary, you get other words as its definition. Those other words can themselves be looked up, yielding more words — and so on forever. The meaning never "bottoms out" in anything non-linguistic. LLMs are essentially very elaborate dictionaries: given a word or phrase, they produce other words and phrases. If dictionary definitions give you meaning, then LLMs give you meaning. But dictionary definitions don't give you genuine meaning — they give you a verbal paraphrase that only makes sense if you already understand the language. LLMs face the same problem at much greater scale.
