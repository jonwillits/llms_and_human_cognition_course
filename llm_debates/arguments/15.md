Reasoning is a functional capacity: a system reasons when it reliably produces valid conclusions from premises, identifies errors in inference, discovers solutions to problems it has not seen before, and adjusts its inferential approach based on the structure of the problem rather than surface features. The question of how the system does this — whether through discrete rules or through learned representations — is a question about implementation, not about whether reasoning is occurring. By the functional standard, LLMs demonstrably reason. They correctly solve syllogisms, identify fallacies in arguments, produce valid multi-step proofs, and generalize inferential strategies to novel problem types. If we define reasoning by its functional signature — valid inference that generalizes across problem structures — then LLMs reason.

Human reasoning is also implemented in a neural network — the brain. Brains do not use explicit logical rules stored in dedicated data structures; they use distributed patterns of activation across billions of neurons, shaped by experience and learning. If "implemented in a learned neural network" disqualified a system from genuinely reasoning, it would disqualify human brains. LLMs are also learned neural networks. So the same argument that shows human brains can reason shows that LLMs can reason.

Genuine reasoning is not about getting the right answer — it is about getting the right answer for the right reasons, in a way that generalizes systematically across structurally similar problems. A system that finds the answer to a reasoning problem by retrieving relevant patterns from training data is not reasoning, even when it succeeds, because its success is fragile in a particular way: change the surface features of the problem — use different names, different cover stories, different notation — and performance degrades in ways that would not occur in a system that had understood the logical structure. This is exactly the empirical signature LLMs show. Their "reasoning" is surface-sensitive in ways that genuine rule-governed inference is not. That fragility is diagnostic: it tells us that what's happening is pattern retrieval, not reasoning.

LLMs were designed and trained to predict text — specifically, to generate the next token in a sequence by learning statistical patterns across a massive corpus. That is the entirety of what they are optimized to do. Reasoning, by contrast, is a specific cognitive capacity that requires its own dedicated learning or development — it is not a byproduct of text prediction. Since LLMs were never trained to reason, and reasoning is not a consequence of text-prediction training, LLMs cannot genuinely reason. Their outputs may look like reasoning because reasoning appears in their training data, but producing text that resembles reasoning is not the same as reasoning.
