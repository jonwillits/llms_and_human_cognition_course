The rate-limiting step in many areas of science is not data collection or experimentation — it is synthesis. A working scientist in almost any field faces a literature too vast to read comprehensively, written across disciplinary boundaries using different terminologies for overlapping concepts. The result is that important connections go unmade, relevant findings go undiscovered, and researchers spend enormous amounts of time on information retrieval rather than thinking. LLMs can compress this bottleneck dramatically. They can synthesize hundreds of papers into structured summaries, identify conceptual overlaps across disciplines, flag contradictions in the literature, and generate candidate hypotheses based on patterns the human researcher would not have encountered. The cognitive work of synthesis and connection-making is exactly where human researchers are most constrained by time and memory, and it is exactly where LLMs' ability to process vast amounts of text is most useful. The human researcher remains responsible for evaluating, testing, and extending whatever the LLM surfaces — but they can now do so from a much richer starting point.

Science is not just constrained by the pace of discovery — it is constrained by who can participate. Doing original research currently requires extensive training, institutional affiliation, access to expensive tools, and fluency in the conventions of scientific writing and peer review. These barriers exclude many capable people and concentrate scientific production in a small number of wealthy institutions and countries. LLMs lower several of these barriers simultaneously. They help non-native English speakers write publication-quality prose. They help researchers at under-resourced institutions access and synthesize literature they could not otherwise navigate. They help early-career scientists learn methodological conventions. They help interdisciplinary researchers translate between fields. None of this replaces scientific training, but it reduces the degree to which accident of birth, geography, and institutional prestige determine who gets to contribute. A more inclusive scientific community is, all else equal, one that generates more diverse hypotheses, catches more errors, and produces more robust knowledge.

LLMs generate text that is optimized for plausibility — for sounding like a correct, well-informed answer — rather than for being true. In casual conversation, the difference between plausible and true may not matter much. In science, it is the difference between discovery and error. LLMs hallucinate citations that do not exist. They report findings that were never published. They synthesize literatures in ways that smooth over genuine disagreements, producing summaries that feel authoritative but misrepresent the state of knowledge. The danger is not that these errors are obvious — it is that they are precisely the kind of errors that are hardest to detect. A fabricated citation looks exactly like a real one. A subtly distorted finding reads like a correct summary. A researcher who incorporates these errors into their work propagates them into the scientific record, where they may persist undetected. The cost of undetected error in science is very high, and LLMs are machines for producing undetected errors at scale.

Genuine scientific discovery often requires breaking from the consensus view — proposing a mechanism no one has considered, questioning an assumption everyone takes for granted, or noticing that the dominant framework cannot explain an anomalous finding. LLMs are structurally biased against this kind of thinking. They are trained on the existing literature, which means they reflect and reinforce the paradigms, assumptions, and framings that currently dominate each field. When asked to generate hypotheses, they produce hypotheses that are consistent with prevailing views. When asked to identify important research questions, they identify questions the field is already asking. When asked to synthesize, they smooth over disagreements rather than sharpen them. This is not a bug — it is a direct consequence of being trained to predict the most likely next token given the existing distribution of scientific text. A tool that reliably produces the consensus view is useful for many things, but it is the opposite of what is needed for the kind of thinking that produces paradigm shifts, surprises, and genuine new knowledge.
