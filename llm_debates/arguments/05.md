The core problem of the modern information environment is not too little information — it is too much, arriving too fast, from too many sources, with no built-in mechanism for distinguishing what matters from what doesn't. This is a filtering problem, and it is one that human cognition is poorly equipped to solve unaided. We have limited working memory, strong recency bias, and an attentional system that evolved for environments with far less informational density. LLMs can function as cognitive filters: they can summarize long documents so you don't have to read everything to find what's relevant, compress meeting transcripts into action items, extract the key claims from a research paper, and flag contradictions across sources. This is not replacing human judgment — it is structuring the input so that human judgment has something manageable to work with. A person who uses an LLM to reduce a thousand pages to the fifty that actually matter for their decision is not thinking less — they are thinking about the right things.

One of the most underappreciated uses of LLMs is as a tool for slowing down. In a media environment optimized for reactive engagement — hot takes, doom-scrolling, outrage cycles — LLMs offer something structurally different: a conversational partner that can help you think through a topic before you react to it. You can ask an LLM to present multiple perspectives on a news story before you form an opinion. You can ask it to identify what you would need to know before taking a position. You can use it to stress-test your reasoning by asking for the strongest counterargument. None of this requires the LLM to be wise or unbiased — it just requires it to generate relevant considerations on demand. The effect is to insert a deliberative step between encountering information and reacting to it, which is exactly what reflective thinking requires and what the current attention economy systematically discourages.

Understanding something requires cognitive effort — the sustained attention, active processing, and mental struggle that produce genuine comprehension. This is not an unfortunate cost of learning; it is the mechanism by which learning occurs. Cognitive science research on "desirable difficulties" shows that conditions that make learning feel harder in the moment — generation rather than passive reading, testing rather than re-studying, interleaving rather than blocking — produce better long-term retention and transfer. LLMs systematically remove these desirable difficulties. They provide answers instead of requiring generation. They deliver polished summaries instead of making you struggle through primary sources. They eliminate the effortful processing that produces deep understanding. The result is that information feels more accessible — users report learning more — while actual comprehension and retention decline. LLMs create a cognitive environment that optimizes for the feeling of understanding while undermining the process that produces it.

Before LLMs, producing persuasive, well-crafted content at scale required substantial human effort — writing, editing, strategizing, deploying. This effort constraint served as a natural brake on the volume of attention-competing content in the information environment. LLMs remove that brake. They allow anyone to produce polished, audience-targeted, persuasive text at near-zero marginal cost. The consequence is a massive increase in the volume of content competing for finite human attention — more marketing copy, more AI-generated articles, more synthetic social media posts, more strategically crafted messages designed to capture engagement. Human attention has not expanded; it is the same limited resource it has always been. But the volume of content competing for it has exploded. The result is a more crowded, more competitive, more manipulative information environment in which it becomes harder — not easier — to find, evaluate, and engage with information that is genuinely worth one's attention.
