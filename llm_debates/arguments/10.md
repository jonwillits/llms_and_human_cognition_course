Training and deploying frontier LLMs requires enormous quantities of electricity, water for data-center cooling, and specialized hardware whose manufacture has its own environmental and material costs. The trajectory of these demands is upward, driven by continued model scaling, expanding deployment, the competitive dynamics among developers, and growing integration of LLMs into applications that were previously served by less compute-intensive systems. Projected growth in AI compute and data-center buildout over the next decade represents a major new source of energy and water demand at precisely the time when the global energy system needs to be decarbonizing rapidly to meet climate targets. New demand of this scale, on this timeline, works directly against the carbon-reduction trajectory the climate science community has described as necessary. Whatever the long-run equilibrium, the transition the current trajectory implies is one that makes decarbonization harder and more expensive rather than easier.

The environmental costs of LLMs are not borne where the benefits accrue. Data centers draw water in regions that are already water-stressed — the American Southwest, parts of Chile, parts of East Asia — where they compete with agriculture, ecosystems, and drinking water. Grid infrastructure and generation siting disproportionately burden communities near transmission corridors and power plants. Rare-earth mining, semiconductor fabrication, and electronic waste disposal are concentrated in specific countries and specific communities within those countries, often ones with limited regulatory protection. The benefits of LLMs — more capable tools for paying users, economic returns to developing companies, productivity gains in wealthy economies — are distributed very differently. A technology whose costs fall on people who did not consent to bear them and do not primarily benefit from the outputs is engaging in environmental injustice, regardless of whether the aggregate benefit-cost calculation works out favorably for the global economy overall.

LLM compute per unit of useful output has fallen dramatically in a short period. Architectural improvements (better attention mechanisms, mixture-of-experts designs, improved optimizers), quantization, distillation to smaller models, hardware efficiency gains, and better training methods have reduced the energy required for comparable capability by orders of magnitude over just a few years. Commercial pressure rewards efficiency because compute is one of the largest operating costs — the incentive structure of the industry itself pulls in the direction of lower environmental cost per task. At the same time, LLMs are often substituting for activities that have their own environmental footprints: manual research, commuting to meetings, printed materials, other computational tools, duplicated human work. An accurate environmental accounting has to compare the full substitution picture rather than treating LLM compute as purely additional demand with no offset. When that accounting is done properly, the picture is far more favorable than raw compute-growth numbers suggest.

The environmental footprint of LLMs is a cost; the effect of LLMs on broader environmental optimization is a potential benefit. LLMs and related AI systems are already contributing to materials discovery (including catalysts for carbon capture and more efficient batteries), grid management and demand response, supply-chain optimization, building energy efficiency, scientific modeling of climate systems, and early-stage design of low-emissions industrial processes. The efficiency gains these domains support are often orders of magnitude larger than the direct compute footprint of the AI systems used to produce them — a single improvement in industrial catalysis or a single grid-optimization deployment can save more energy than is used by the AI systems that enabled the discovery. Assessing LLMs' net environmental effect requires looking at both sides of this ledger, not only at the direct compute costs. When the indirect gains are plausibly larger than the direct costs, treating only the direct side as decisive misrepresents the overall balance.
