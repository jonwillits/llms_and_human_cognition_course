Intelligence is not a mystical inner glow that some things have and others don't. It is a description of what a system can do: perceive information, generalize across situations, solve problems it has not encountered before, and adapt its behavior based on outcomes. By every behavioral standard we apply to other entities, LLMs pass. They translate between languages. They explain complex scientific concepts to a non-specialist. They write legal arguments, compose poetry, and debug code — often better than most humans. If the same performance in a biology student would lead us to call that student intelligent, intellectual consistency requires us to apply the same description to the system producing equivalent outputs. To refuse to do so is to define intelligence in whatever way makes our preferred answer come out right.

The best-validated measure of intelligence we have is the IQ test and related standardized assessments — SAT, GRE, professional licensing exams, medical boards. These tests were designed specifically to capture the construct of intelligence independent of domain-specific knowledge. LLMs score at or above the 90th human percentile on many of these assessments. Humans who score at that level are, by universal consensus, considered highly intelligent. The same evidence that supports calling those humans intelligent supports calling LLMs intelligent. To apply the standard in one case and not the other is arbitrary.

Intelligence is not simply output quality — it is a relationship between a system, its goals, and the world. An intelligent system does not merely produce appropriate responses; it has something it is trying to accomplish, and it structures its behavior around achieving that. This is what separates a thermostat from a chess player and a chess player from a scientist. LLMs have no goals of their own. They have no model of what they are trying to accomplish beyond completing the current input context. Their impressive outputs emerge from next-token prediction over learned statistical regularities — a process that has no more to do with intelligence than a sophisticated autocomplete function. Whatever we call what LLMs do, it should not be intelligence, because the machinery behind the outputs is categorically different from the machinery that intelligent behavior requires.

No one has ever called a calculator intelligent, even a very fast one. Calculators process symbols according to rules and produce correct outputs — they just do it for arithmetic rather than language. LLMs are doing fundamentally the same thing, at far greater scale and over a much richer input space. But scale and complexity cannot by themselves create intelligence; water is still water whether you have a teaspoon or an ocean. If we're not willing to say calculators are intelligent, intellectual consistency should prevent us from saying LLMs are intelligent, since they're just much more complicated calculators.
