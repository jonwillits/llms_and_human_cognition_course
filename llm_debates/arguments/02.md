LLMs are trained on enormous corpora of copyrighted creative and academic work that was acquired without the consent of the people who wrote it. Novelists, journalists, scholars, poets, and working writers of every kind had their work scraped, processed, and fed into commercial systems that now generate content competing with the very works they were trained on. When an LLM produces output, that output is causally downstream of those inputs — without the training data, the capability would not exist. Commercial AI developers are generating billions of dollars of value from an asset they did not license. Calling this "learning" rather than "appropriation" is a rhetorical move, not a legal or ethical one. The use is unauthorized, the value is extracted, and the people whose work made the system possible are not compensated. That is the structure of content theft.

Academic and professional integrity systems exist for a specific reason: credentials, grades, and evaluations are supposed to reflect what the person being evaluated can actually do. A diploma means something because the person who earned it produced the work behind it. A legal brief with a partner's name on it is supposed to represent that partner's reasoning. A peer-reviewed paper is supposed to reflect its authors' thinking. When students submit LLM-drafted essays as their own work, or professionals put their names on LLM-generated analyses without disclosure, the signal these systems are supposed to transmit becomes false. The work is not what it is presented as being. This is the plain definition of plagiarism — representing someone or something else's output as your own — and the fact that the "someone" is an AI system does not change its function as deception about the source of the reasoning.

The intellectual traditions we depend on have always been built by people learning from, being influenced by, and synthesizing the work of others. Academic writing is saturated with ideas, framings, and phrasings absorbed from prior work. Creative writers read thousands of books before producing their own. Lawyers build arguments from precedent. Scientists replicate and extend prior methods. In every one of these cases, we do not call the result theft — we call it learning, influence, synthesis, or transformation. LLMs do something structurally similar at greater scale: they extract statistical regularities from prior work and generate novel outputs that do not reproduce the inputs verbatim. The same norms that already distinguish "building on prior work" from "plagiarism" should apply here too. Applying a stricter standard to LLMs than we apply to any human writer who does the same thing requires either an independent principled distinction or a blanket update of the norms to cover both cases. Asserting that LLMs are theft without providing that distinction is the rhetorical move.

Integrity norms exist to prevent specific harms — deception about who did the work, fraudulent credentials, unearned credit, and the suppression of other people's contributions. Those harms are real and worth protecting against. But they are not prevented by declaring one entire category of tool use off limits. They are prevented by requiring disclosure, designing assessments that evaluate demonstrated capacity rather than textual artifacts, and treating attribution as a serious practice. A student who discloses that an LLM helped draft their outline, engages substantively with the material, and produces work that reflects their understanding has done nothing to produce any of the harms the norms were designed to prevent. Calling this "plagiarism" conflates tool use with deception and extends the norms beyond their own rationale. The correct response to LLMs is to update the enforcement mechanisms — disclosure requirements, oral defenses, in-class writing, competency-based evaluation — not to redefine every use of a writing tool as a violation.
