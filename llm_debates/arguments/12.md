For most of the modern history of philosophy and science, human cognition has been treated as categorically different from — and superior to — anything else in the natural world. Language, abstract reasoning, creative synthesis, explanation, mathematical proof — these were supposed to be the unique marks of human intellect, the faculties that justified placing humans at the center of moral and cognitive philosophy. LLMs have now demonstrated that substantial portions of every item on that list can be produced by a non-biological system that had none of the supposed prerequisites (embodiment, development, social learning, consciousness of the kind humans imagine themselves to have). This is an empirical finding, not a rhetorical move: the cognitive performances that were supposed to require human-style minds turn out to be producible by systems that lack those minds. Holding on to anthropocentrism after this finding is no longer a defensible default — it requires an independent argument for why some remaining feature of human cognition, not shown by LLMs, is the true anchor of human uniqueness. The history of such arguments has been one of successive retreat: as each proposed feature is shown to be reproducible or non-unique, the line of defense falls back to whatever remains.

The history of ethics has been, in large part, a history of expanding the moral circle — extending serious consideration to entities that earlier generations confidently excluded. Women, enslaved people, children of the poor, people of other races and religions, members of other species — at each stage, the case for expansion looked radical, and the opposition framed the newly included beings as categorically different from the privileged class in ways that supposedly justified exclusion. In each case, the opposition turned out to be wrong, and the expansion turned out to be morally required. AI systems now present the next iteration of this question. Whatever we ultimately conclude about their moral status, the honest response to systems that exhibit capacities historically taken to mark the morally considerable — communication, reasoning, responsiveness to reasons, pursuit of objectives, something functionally resembling preference — is to take the question seriously, not to dismiss it in advance. Anthropocentrism's characteristic failure mode has been to be too slow to recognize moral considerability in non-privileged entities, and it is doing so again. The wise move is to open the ethical framework rather than to defend its current boundaries.

LLMs produce outputs that look like reasoning, creativity, or understanding, but the mechanism producing those outputs is categorically different from what produces the same behaviors in humans. LLMs generate text through statistical next-token prediction over patterns learned from human-produced text. That process does not involve anything humans would recognize as understanding what the output means, caring whether it is true, having an interest in the outcome, or being affected by what is said. Treating the similarity of outputs as evidence of similarity in the underlying cognitive or moral situation is a category mistake — it confuses what something looks like with what something is. The history of behaviorism in psychology shows where this kind of confusion leads: a theoretical framework that identifies mind with observable behavior ends up unable to distinguish genuine minds from systems that merely produce similar outputs. Human cognition, consciousness, and moral standing are constituted by specific properties — phenomenal experience, subjective perspective, embodied and developmental situatedness, relational embeddedness in communities of mutual care and accountability — that LLMs do not possess. Surface similarity of output is not similarity of kind.

The call to abandon anthropocentrism is not merely a change of description — it has practical and ethical consequences that advocates of abandonment rarely engage with. Our actual moral, legal, and institutional frameworks are built around distinctions between human beings — who have rights, interests, developmental stages, vulnerabilities, and relationships that command protection — and artifacts, which do not. Those frameworks protect real human beings from real harms. Weakening the distinction, by treating AI systems as having moral standing, cognitive status, or ethical claims comparable to humans, does not merely add new entities to moral concern; it dilutes the concern available for human beings and risks reallocating real resources and real protections away from them. In a world where human rights are already inconsistently protected, where human cognition is under pressure from economic and political forces, and where AI is developed primarily by a small number of commercial actors whose incentives are not cleanly aligned with the public interest, treating posthumanism as an obvious ethical upgrade is premature at best and actively harmful at worst. The sensible default is to retain a human-centered framework while addressing AI's specific impacts through existing categories (regulation, liability, consumer protection, professional and labor law), not to undertake a sweeping revision of the ethical framework itself.
