Every major cognitive technology — writing, printing, calculators, search engines — was met with warnings that it would erode human mental abilities. Socrates worried that writing would destroy memory. Critics of calculators warned that students would lose arithmetic fluency. In every case, the predicted catastrophe did not materialize in the way critics feared: people adapted, skills shifted rather than disappeared, and the net cognitive capacity of individuals and societies increased. LLMs are the latest entry in this sequence. They automate routine cognitive work — summarizing, drafting, organizing information — in the same way calculators automated routine arithmetic. If the pattern holds, humans will adapt by reallocating cognitive effort toward skills that LLMs cannot replace, and the net result will be beneficial.

Cognitive load theory, one of the best-supported frameworks in educational psychology, demonstrates that working memory is sharply limited and that performance on complex tasks degrades when cognitive resources are consumed by routine sub-tasks. Offloading routine operations to external tools frees working memory for the high-level reasoning that actually matters. This is why we let students use calculators during physics exams — not because arithmetic doesn't matter, but because the physics reasoning is what we're trying to develop, and arithmetic consumes cognitive resources that would otherwise support it. LLMs do the same thing at a higher level: by handling summarization, information retrieval, and first-draft generation, they free cognitive resources for evaluation, synthesis, and creative thinking. The result is not skill loss — it is better allocation of a scarce resource.

Cognitive skills — writing clearly, reasoning through complex problems, synthesizing information from multiple sources — are maintained through deliberate practice. This is not a metaphor; it is one of the best-replicated findings in cognitive science and skill acquisition research. Skills that are not practiced degrade, and skills that are practiced below the threshold required for maintenance atrophy over time. When people use LLMs to draft their writing, summarize their reading, or structure their arguments, they are removing precisely the practice that maintains those skills. The LLM output may be acceptable or even good, but the user is no longer doing the cognitive work that keeps their own abilities sharp. Over time, this produces a predictable outcome: the skills most frequently offloaded to LLMs will be the skills that degrade most. This is not speculative — it is what the science of skill acquisition predicts whenever practice is removed.

The most dangerous form of skill loss is not losing the ability to perform a task — it is losing the ability to recognize when a task has been performed badly. Metacognition — the capacity to monitor your own understanding, detect errors in reasoning, and calibrate your confidence to your actual knowledge — is a skill that depends on having done the underlying cognitive work yourself. When people write their own arguments, they develop an intuitive sense for when an argument is weak. When they do their own literature reviews, they develop a feel for when a source is unreliable. When they solve their own problems, they learn to recognize when they are stuck versus when they are making progress. LLMs bypass all of this. A student who receives an LLM-generated summary has no basis for evaluating whether the summary is accurate, because they never did the reading that would give them that basis. The result is not just skill loss in the narrow sense — it is the erosion of the monitoring systems that would let you detect that something has gone wrong. You lose not just the ability to do the work, but the ability to notice that the work hasn't been done well.
