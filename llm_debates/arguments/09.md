LLMs learn from the text that exists on the internet, and that text is massively skewed. English alone makes up the majority of the training data for most frontier models — often by an order of magnitude or more over any other single language. A handful of additional high-resource languages (Mandarin, Spanish, French, German, Japanese) make up most of the remainder. Most of the world's ~7,000 languages are functionally invisible in these systems: they are either absent entirely or represented so poorly that the model's outputs are error-prone, stilted, or culturally off-key. As LLMs become more central to writing, education, professional work, and information access, the languages they handle well will be reinforced as the languages of consequential use, while the languages they handle poorly will be increasingly excluded from those high-leverage contexts. This does not correct existing linguistic inequality — it amplifies it, because the technology routes cultural and economic opportunity toward the languages it happens to perform well in.

Even within high-resource languages, LLM output converges on a narrow stylistic register: fluent, hedged, mildly formal, emotionally neutral, structurally standardized. The hedging, the balanced paragraph structure, the predictable sentence openings, the careful avoidance of strong idiomatic or dialectal forms — these are the fingerprints of models trained on majority-register text and tuned through reinforcement learning to be broadly acceptable. As people increasingly use LLMs to draft, edit, translate, and polish their writing, the written record drifts toward this register. Regional dialect, idiosyncratic voice, generational slang, culturally specific rhetorical traditions, code-switching, and the productive roughness of actual human expression are smoothed away in favor of the model-mediated standard. The result is cultural homogenization produced not by coercion but by convenience: the texture of human expression compresses toward whatever the models produce most reliably.

Every major technology for translation, literacy support, and linguistic preservation has historically favored high-resource languages, because the commercial and scholarly investment has gone where the economic return was. Machine translation for decades primarily served a dozen languages. Digital literacy tools, pedagogical materials, transcription tools, and orthographic standardization efforts have similarly concentrated their resources on dominant languages. Against this baseline, LLMs are the first technology in which a speaker of a low-resource language can plausibly use cheap, general-purpose tools to translate into and out of dominant languages, generate pedagogical materials, transcribe oral corpora, produce literacy content, and write in their own language with at least some degree of support. The capability in low-resource languages is genuinely weaker than in English — no one denies this. But the trajectory is expansion where there was almost none before, and the cost of linguistic preservation and revitalization work has fallen dramatically for communities that previously had to do it all manually.

Every major communication technology has been accused of homogenizing language. Print did; radio did; television did; standardized education did; internet English did. In each case, actual linguistic behavior turned out to be far more flexible than the fear anticipated: local variation persisted, new forms emerged, dialects continued to evolve, and the supposed homogenizing pressure coexisted with ongoing linguistic creativity. LLMs are powerful, but they are also tools whose outputs users can reshape in ways that no previous broadcast or print technology allowed. They can be prompted for specific dialects, fine-tuned on community corpora, deployed with retrieval over minority-register sources, and steered away from their default styles. The homogenization concern treats LLM defaults as fixed when they are, in fact, among the most easily modified aspects of the technology — the whole point of prompt engineering and fine-tuning is that the system can be pointed at the kind of output the user actually wants.
