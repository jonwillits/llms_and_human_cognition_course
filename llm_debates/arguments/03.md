The global mental health treatment gap is staggering: the World Health Organization estimates that more than 75% of people with mental health conditions in low- and middle-income countries receive no treatment at all. In high-income countries, waitlists stretch months, costs are prohibitive, and geographic access is uneven. LLMs do not fix this — they are not therapists and should not be treated as such. But they can meaningfully narrow the gap by providing psychoeducation, guided self-reflection, coping skill practice, and crisis triage at a scale and cost that human services cannot match. A person who uses an LLM to practice cognitive restructuring techniques between therapy sessions, or who uses one to articulate their feelings before deciding whether to seek professional help, is better served than a person with no resources at all. The relevant comparison is not "LLM versus expert therapist" — it is "LLM versus nothing."

One of the most significant barriers to seeking mental health support is stigma — the fear of being judged, pathologized, or seen as weak. This is especially acute for men, adolescents, people in cultures where mental health is heavily stigmatized, and people dealing with topics they find shameful (addiction, trauma, relationship difficulties, suicidal ideation). LLMs offer something that no human provider can: a conversational partner that does not judge, does not remember unless asked, does not belong to the user's social world, and is available at 3 AM when the crisis hits. For people who would never call a hotline, never walk into a therapist's office, and never confide in a friend, an LLM may be the first and only context in which they articulate what they are experiencing. This is not a replacement for therapy — it is a pathway that did not previously exist.

Mental health support requires something that LLMs structurally cannot provide: the ability to reliably detect when a person is in danger and respond with clinical judgment calibrated to that person's situation. A therapist recognizes when a client's "I'm fine" is not fine. A crisis counselor knows when suicidal ideation has shifted from passive to active. A psychiatrist notices when a patient's improving mood signals mania rather than recovery. LLMs have no clinical training, no understanding of the person's history, no ability to read vocal tone or body language, and no mechanism for adjusting responses based on clinical risk. When they encounter a person in crisis, they generate a response optimized for plausibility, not safety. For low-stakes emotional reflection this may be benign, but for the populations most drawn to anonymous, low-barrier support — people in crisis, with serious mental illness, or with no other resources — the consequences of clinical incompetence can be severe.

Healthy emotional development depends on learning to tolerate distress, regulate emotions internally, and navigate the imperfect, unpredictable nature of human relationships. These capacities develop through experience — through sitting with discomfort, through conflicts that get repaired, through relationships where the other person sometimes disappoints you. LLMs offer something seductively different: a perfectly patient, always-available, endlessly agreeable conversational partner that never gets tired, never pushes back in uncomfortable ways, and never has needs of its own. For someone who is lonely, anxious, or struggling with human relationships, this can feel like exactly what they need. But what it provides is not connection — it is a simulation of connection that removes the very friction that makes real relationships therapeutic. If people substitute LLM interaction for the difficult, messy work of human connection, they may develop emotional dependence on a system that cannot reciprocate, while their capacity for genuine human relationship atrophies.
