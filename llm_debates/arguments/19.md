Human understanding of concepts like "balance," "force," "weight," "temperature," and "pain" is not learned from reading descriptions of those experiences — it is acquired through direct sensorimotor interaction with the world. An infant understands "heavy" not by being told what heavy means, but by reaching for an object and having their arm pulled down. Simulation studies and lesion data suggest that understanding these concepts involves activating the same sensorimotor systems that underlie the experiences themselves. This means that concept understanding, for these foundational concepts, is constitutively tied to embodied systems. A system that has never had a body, never felt weight, never been pulled by gravity, is missing the representational scaffolding that grounds these concepts. No matter how many sentences about weight it processes, the sensorimotor representations aren't there.

Studies of congenitally blind individuals show that they have impaired performance on some visual-spatial tasks compared to sighted individuals, despite having full access to language that describes visual and spatial concepts. This demonstrates that linguistic access to a concept is not sufficient for full understanding — the sensory experience is also required. LLMs are in an even more extreme position: they lack not just visual experience but all sensory experience whatsoever. We should therefore expect their understanding of sensory and spatial concepts to be even more impaired than that of congenitally blind individuals.

The text that LLMs train on was written by embodied humans who had the full range of sensorimotor experience. When those humans write about weight, balance, warmth, and pain, they encode their embodied knowledge in the linguistic structure they use — in which terms co-occur, in which inferences they draw, in which contexts they find descriptions appropriate. The linguistic record is a massive lossy compression of embodied human experience. If that compression retains enough of the relevant structure, then a system that learns from the text can reconstruct something functionally equivalent to embodied understanding — not by having experiences, but by learning the representation of those experiences as encoded in language. This is an empirical claim, and the evidence so far is surprisingly supportive: LLMs do acquire spatial representations, causal representations, and conceptual structures that resemble those of embodied agents more than we would expect from naive views of distributional learning.

LLMs can answer questions about physical scenarios they have never experienced. Ask an LLM what happens if you put a glass of water on a tilted table, and it will correctly predict the glass slides and falls. Ask it what you feel when you run your hand along rough sandpaper, and it will describe the texture accurately. If embodiment were truly required for understanding physical concepts, LLMs would fail on these questions — they have no physical experience to draw on. Their success shows that language-only learning is sufficient.
