Training frontier LLMs requires billions of dollars of compute, datasets no individual actor can assemble, and specialized engineering talent that only a handful of organizations possess. The capability to produce these systems is structurally concentrated: there is no meaningful path by which a mid-size company, a university, or a nation-state without a major industrial base can independently produce frontier-grade models. And because the frontier sets the terms — it determines what is possible, what is expected, and what downstream products can do — whoever controls the frontier controls the infrastructure of an increasingly consequential technology. This matters because LLMs are becoming embedded in education, work, information retrieval, healthcare, law, and government services. Infrastructural control of a technology that is becoming pervasive is a form of power, regardless of whether those holding it have good intentions. The checks that diversity and competition normally provide are absent when infrastructure is held by a handful of actors.

The most subtle and perhaps most important form of concentration LLMs produce is epistemic. Hundreds of millions of users — including an increasing share of students, professionals, researchers, and decision-makers — now route their information retrieval, explanation, summarization, and analysis through the same small handful of models. Whatever framings those models default to, whatever omissions their training data produced, whatever subtle errors their fine-tuning installed, get propagated through the information ecosystem at scale. Intellectual progress and collective error-correction depend on diverse, independent perspectives pushing against each other. When a significant fraction of cognitive labor is mediated by a few commercial systems that share training data, share RLHF conventions, and share commercial incentives, the independence that makes error-correction possible is structurally reduced. This is a form of power that does not require coercion to be dangerous — it just requires default reliance.

For most of human history, access to expert-level cognitive services — a competent legal memo, a coherent research summary, a well-structured persuasive essay, clear technical explanations, working code — has been gated by education, wealth, and geography. Most of the world's population has had no such access. LLMs have changed this in a way no previous technology has. A first-generation college student preparing for law school, a small-business owner drafting contracts without a lawyer, a non-native English writer competing for jobs, a nurse in a rural clinic searching for differential diagnoses, a teacher writing lesson plans in a under-resourced school — all of them now have tools that previously required hiring a specialist. The capability floor has risen across exactly those populations that had little capability before. Real concerns about concentration at the frontier must be weighed honestly against the distributional redistribution that has already happened at the user level — and that second effect is much larger than the first.

The current state of LLM development is not monolithic. Multiple independent frontier developers — OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, Chinese labs, and well-resourced academic consortia — compete fiercely, and the result is rapid capability gains, aggressive price reductions, and no single actor able to dictate terms. Open-weight models from Meta, Mistral, and others can be downloaded and run locally, fine-tuned on specific domains, and deployed outside the control of any frontier developer; their capabilities match closed-source frontier performance on many practical tasks within a 6–18 month lag. Smaller companies, universities, and even capable individuals can build customized LLM-powered products without depending on a single gatekeeper. Concentration critiques assume a structural condition — captive users, single-provider dominance, monopoly pricing power — that the actual competitive dynamics of the LLM market do not satisfy.
