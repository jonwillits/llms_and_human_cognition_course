AI capability is advancing quickly, and our ability to ensure that these systems reliably pursue goals we want them to pursue lags behind their capability gains. Alignment is an unsolved technical problem — we do not yet know how to specify human values, verify that a system has internalized them, or maintain those commitments through continued training and deployment. As systems become more capable at planning, acting in the world, and pursuing open-ended objectives, the consequences of misspecification scale with capability. A system that is both very capable and misaligned can produce outcomes that are catastrophic and irreversible, especially if it is deployed in domains where humans cannot easily monitor, correct, or shut it down. We do not have to assume malevolence on anyone's part — the danger comes from the structural gap between capability growth and alignment research, and from commercial and geopolitical pressures that reward deployment even when safety is unresolved.

Even setting aside the technical alignment problem, advanced AI creates a second, possibly larger pathway to catastrophe: it concentrates unprecedented capability in the hands of whoever controls the systems. A small group — a lab, a corporation, a government, or a non-state actor — that obtains decisive AI capability before others could use it to entrench a political, economic, or military position from which recovery is impossible. History provides few precedents for what happens when one actor acquires durable, order-of-magnitude advantages over everyone else in a domain as general as cognition. The consequences do not require misalignment, they do not require malevolence, and they do not require errors — they only require ordinary human incentives (competitive advantage, political control, protection of existing assets) applied to a tool powerful enough to make the resulting imbalance permanent. That is a pathway to civilizational catastrophe that runs through ordinary human behavior, not through misaligned AI.

Humanity has a very long track record of confidently predicting civilizational catastrophe from new technologies, and those predictions have an unusually poor hit rate. Nuclear power was supposed to end civilization; genetic engineering was supposed to end it; the internet was supposed to end it; social media was supposed to end it; synthetic biology is still supposed to end it. Each of those predictions came from smart, informed people who laid out detailed scenarios. In each case, real harms occurred — some of them serious — but the civilizational-collapse outcomes that were actually forecast did not materialize in the form or magnitude predicted. AI doom scenarios inherit the same structural features: they project current trends far into the future, assume specific failure modes without strong evidence, and treat conjunctions of low-probability events (rapid capability jump + alignment failure + deployment in high-leverage context + no corrective response) as if they were near-certain. When the outside view is applied honestly — what fraction of confidently predicted civilizational catastrophes from emerging technology have actually been realized in their predicted form? — the base rate is very low, and the appropriate prior on AI doom should reflect that.

The same properties that generate AI-doom scenarios — enormous capability increases across general cognitive tasks — generate equally realistic scenarios for massive positive transformation. Every major challenge facing humanity is cognitively bottlenecked in ways AI is structured to help with: medical research where we lack enough theorists; education where we lack enough trained teachers; scientific discovery where we cannot read everything or synthesize across fields; legal, medical, and financial expertise that most of the world cannot afford. AI-assisted drug discovery, materials science for clean energy, personalized education at scale, decision support in governance, diagnostic access for populations with no physicians — these are not science fiction, they are already beginning, and their realistic trajectory over the coming decades points toward gains in human welfare on a scale comparable to the industrial and agricultural revolutions. A full accounting of AI's long-run impact has to put these possibilities on the ledger alongside the risks, and when it does, the expected-value picture tilts strongly positive — not because the risks are zero, but because the upside, when seriously estimated, is historically enormous.
