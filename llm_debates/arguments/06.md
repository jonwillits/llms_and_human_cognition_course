Fact-checking has always been bottlenecked by human labor. A professional fact-checker can verify a handful of claims per day; a political speech, viral tweet, or news broadcast may contain dozens. The result is that the vast majority of public claims go unchecked, and the claims that do get checked are checked slowly — often long after they have spread. LLMs change this calculus by automating the most time-consuming parts of the verification workflow: identifying what is being claimed, locating relevant sources, checking whether the claim is consistent with the evidence, and flagging discrepancies. They do not replace human editorial judgment, but they can reduce the time per claim from hours to minutes, allowing fact-checking organizations to scale their coverage by orders of magnitude. If the limiting factor in combating misinformation has been the speed and scale of human verification, then a tool that accelerates verification addresses the bottleneck directly.

Misinformation works because it exploits predictable vulnerabilities in human reasoning — emotional appeals, motivated reasoning, source credibility shortcuts, and the tendency to accept claims that feel true without checking. These vulnerabilities are well-understood by propagandists and poorly understood by the general public. LLMs can help close this literacy gap. They can be used to teach people how persuasion techniques work — by generating examples of manipulative rhetoric and asking the user to identify what makes them effective. They can help individuals evaluate the credibility of specific claims by walking through the reasoning step by step. They can serve as on-demand critical thinking partners that model the kind of skeptical evaluation most people never learned. This is not about LLMs being unbiased oracles — it is about them being tools that make the structure of persuasion visible to people who would otherwise be unaware of it.

The economics of misinformation have always favored the attacker. Producing a lie is fast and cheap; verifying it is slow and expensive. A fabricated claim can spread to millions in hours; a careful fact-check might reach thousands over days. LLMs dramatically widen this asymmetry. They reduce the cost of producing convincing, targeted, context-specific misinformation to near zero while leaving the cost of verification essentially unchanged. A single operator can now generate thousands of unique, audience-tailored false narratives — each superficially different enough to evade automated detection, each crafted to appeal to the specific beliefs and anxieties of a target audience. The verification side of the equation has no comparable force multiplier. Checking a claim still requires source retrieval, expert judgment, and contextual assessment — tasks that are partially but not fully automatable. The net effect is that LLMs disproportionately empower the production side of the misinformation economy, widening the gap between the capacity to deceive and the capacity to verify.

The most corrosive effect of LLMs on information ecosystems may not be the misinformation they produce — it may be the doubt they cast on everything else. Once it becomes widely known that any text, image, or audio could have been generated by AI, the default assumption shifts: nothing can be trusted on its face. This is the "liar's dividend" — the benefit that accrues to actual liars when the mere possibility of AI fabrication gives everyone a reason to doubt authentic content. A politician caught on audio saying something damaging can now claim the recording is AI-generated. A whistleblower's document can be dismissed as synthetic. A journalist's investigation can be waved away as possibly fabricated. The paradox is that LLMs undermine trust in information not only by producing false content but by making it rational to distrust true content. The result is an epistemic environment in which trust erodes across the board, verification becomes impossibly expensive, and the shared evidentiary basis for public reasoning collapses.
