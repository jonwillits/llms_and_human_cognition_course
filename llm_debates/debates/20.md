David Marr argued that there are three levels at which any information-processing system can be analyzed: the computational level (what is the system doing and why?), the algorithmic level (how is it doing it?), and the implementational level (what physical substrate realizes the algorithm?). Marr's point was that these levels are partially independent — knowing the computational level answer does not require knowing the implementational answer. Cognitive science has always operated primarily at the computational and algorithmic levels; we do not require knowledge of individual synapse dynamics to do good cognitive psychology. LLMs provide extraordinarily precise and successful models at the computational level — they capture what the cognitive system achieves with remarkable fidelity. That is a genuine scientific contribution at the correct level of analysis for cognitive science.

We have been doing successful cognitive science for decades without mechanistic accounts of the neural implementation of the processes we study. Memory researchers explain forgetting, encoding, and retrieval without knowing which specific synapses are involved. Language researchers explain syntactic priming and ambiguity resolution without knowing which neurons fire. The cognitive level of explanation has been enormously productive without neural-level mechanism. If we accept those explanations as genuine cognitive science, we should accept LLM-based explanations on the same grounds — they operate at the same level of abstraction.

Explanation is not prediction. A model that predicts the acceleration of falling objects with arbitrary precision tells you nothing, by itself, about why objects fall. Newton's model explained falling by positing gravitational force; Einstein's by positing curved spacetime. The prediction was a consequence of the explanation, not the explanation itself. The same distinction applies in cognitive science. An LLM that predicts human reading times doesn't explain them — it demonstrates a correlation between its architecture and human behavior. To explain human reading times, you need an account of the cognitive processes that produce them: how lexical access interacts with syntactic parsing, how working memory load distributes across the sentence, how prediction and integration interact in real time. LLMs predict without explaining, and we should be clear about which one we have.

If we fully understood the computations performed by a human brain — every activation, every weight update, every information transformation — we could, in principle, explain everything about human cognition. This is why neuroscience is fundamental: the brain is the implementation, and full knowledge of the implementation would constitute full explanation. LLMs are analogous: their computations are fully specified. We can, in principle, inspect every parameter and activation. Therefore, LLMs give us something even better than a model of human cognition — they give us a complete computational system where everything is transparent, and understanding that system would constitute explanation of the cognitive phenomena it mimics.
