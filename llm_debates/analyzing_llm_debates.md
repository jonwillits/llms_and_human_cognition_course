## Slide 8

### Title
The Problem With Most AI Debates

### Slide Text
Most public AI discourse clusters into two camps: hype or doom

These positions often function as tribal identities, not reasoned views

Claims are stated with far more confidence than the evidence warrants

This makes genuine learning and belief revision almost impossible

### Speaker Notes
If you've spent any time reading about AI in popular media or on social platforms, you've probably noticed a pattern.
On one side there is breathless enthusiasm — AI will cure cancer, end poverty, solve every problem.
On the other side there is alarm or dismissal — AI is dangerous, meaningless, a threat to humanity or to nothing at all.
These positions often signal group membership more than they reflect careful thinking.

The problem isn't that people have opinions about AI.
The problem is that the confidence people express routinely exceeds what the evidence supports.
People speak with certainty about things that are genuinely uncertain, and they don't update when they should.
That's epistemically bad regardless of which direction it goes.

This tribal structure also makes it nearly impossible to learn from the debates.
If your goal is winning, you're not going to admit uncertainty.
If your goal is signaling, evidence doesn't help you.
And if everyone is performing rather than thinking, no one gets smarter.

## Slide 9

### Title
A Better Goal: Calibrated Reasoning

### Slide Text
The goal is not to win — it is to reason well under uncertainty

Calibrated reasoning means your confidence tracks the strength of the evidence

This requires separating what we know, what we believe, and what we value

Good epistemic practice is compatible with having real, substantive positions

Example: "AI will definitely replace all writers" vs. "AI tools are changing some writing workflows, but strong claims about complete replacement outrun the evidence"

### Speaker Notes
Calibration is a concept from probability and forecasting.
A calibrated reasoner is one whose confidence levels match their actual track record of accuracy — when they say they're 80% sure, they're right about 80% of the time.
In informal debate, it means not claiming more certainty than you have, and not hiding behind false uncertainty when the evidence is actually pretty clear.

Calibrated reasoning is harder than it sounds, because it requires noticing and resisting motivated reasoning.
We naturally evaluate evidence more favorably when it supports what we already believe.
We find reasons to dismiss inconvenient findings.
Being calibrated means applying the same scrutiny to evidence that supports your view as to evidence that challenges it.

Importantly, calibration doesn't mean being wishy-washy or refusing to take positions.
It means holding your positions for the right reasons, with appropriate confidence given the evidence, and being willing to revise when new information warrants it.
You can have strong views and still be epistemically responsible.
That combination is what we're practicing.

## Slide 10

### Title
The Epistemic Analysis Framework

### Slide Text
Step 1: Identify the argument — write out premises and conclusion

Step 2: Evaluate validity and soundness — check logical structure and premise quality

Step 3: Classify the premises — empirical vs. non-empirical

Step 4: Evaluate the existing evidence — quality, scope, and limitations

Step 5: Specify the missing data — what would resolve the open empirical questions

Step 6: Address non-empirical premises — values, concepts, and how to handle disagreement

### Speaker Notes
These six steps form a workflow for any structured debate analysis.
They're sequenced deliberately: first get the argument clearly specified, then evaluate its structure, then dig into its substance.
Steps 1–2 anchor the framework in formal logic.
Steps 3–6 move into epistemology and the philosophy of science.

The logic background is not just decoration.
Argument structure matters because a structurally broken argument can't be fixed by adding more evidence.
If the conclusion doesn't follow from the premises even when the premises are true, the argument fails regardless of how much you know about the topic.

The epistemological steps (3–6) are where most of the substantive work happens for these debates.
Identifying which claims are empirical, evaluating what evidence exists, naming what's missing, and honestly engaging with value disagreements — these are the moves that separate good analysis from just talking.
The framework gives you a checklist so nothing gets skipped.

The full framework is also in the background readings for this unit.
If you want to see how each step connects to the broader literature in informal logic and philosophy of science, those references are worth a look.

## Slide 11

### Title
Step 1: Identify the Argument

### Slide Text
Write out the argument in standard form — do not leave premises implicit

Standard form: Premise 1, Premise 2, therefore Conclusion

Draw an argument diagram to show which premises support which claims

Many apparent disagreements disappear once the argument is written down explicitly

### Speaker Notes
This step looks simple, and people often want to skip it or rush through it.
Don't.
The act of writing an argument out in standard form forces implicit assumptions into the open, and that's where the real action is.
Most arguments in the wild leave their most questionable premises unstated.

Standard form means each premise is a separate numbered sentence, followed by a clearly stated conclusion.
It does not mean flowery language or elaborate framing — the simpler and more direct the better.
If a premise can't be stated as a clean declarative sentence, that's a signal that it's doing more work than it appears.

Argument diagrams are optional but useful, especially when arguments have complex structure.
An argument diagram shows which premises independently support the conclusion and which ones support each other first before supporting the conclusion.
This matters because it tells you which premises are doing the most load-bearing work — those are the ones worth scrutinizing hardest.

A useful test: try writing out the strongest version of the opposing argument in a form that advocates of that position would recognize and accept.
If you can't do it, you probably don't understand the opposing view well enough to critique it effectively.
Steelmanning the other side is not a nicety — it's a precondition for good analysis.

Your starter arguments are written in natural language — the way they actually appear in real discourse, not as formal logical statements.
Part of your Step 1 task is to do this conversion yourself: read the argument, identify what's claimed explicitly, and ask what else has to be true for the conclusion to follow.
The hidden claims are often the most important ones.
We'll look at an example of this right now.

## Slide 12

### Title
Step 1 in Action: Surfacing Hidden Premises

### Slide Text
"AI keeps getting smarter every year. Eventually it will surpass us, and at that point we won't be able to control it."

P1: AI capabilities have improved significantly in recent decades. [stated]

P2: This rate of improvement will continue until AI surpasses human intelligence. [hidden]

P3: A superintelligent AI will necessarily behave in ways humans cannot control. [hidden]

C: Therefore, AI will eventually become uncontrollable.

### Speaker Notes
The natural language version of this argument sounds intuitive and even compelling to many people.
But notice what's not in it: the two most important claims are never stated explicitly.
The argument as written only tells you that AI has improved — the rest is left for you to assume.
Writing it in standard form makes those assumptions visible.

P2 assumes that the trajectory of recent improvement will continue in a straight line until human-level intelligence is reached.
That's a strong empirical claim about how technological progress works — and it's exactly the kind of claim where the evidence is contested and uncertain.
P3 assumes that superintelligent AI would necessarily act in ways humans cannot control — which is precisely what serious AI safety researchers spend their careers arguing about.
Both of those hidden premises are far more questionable than the stated one.

Your starter arguments work the same way — written as they might appear in a think-piece, an op-ed, or a social media thread.
Your job in Step 1 is to do what we just did here: read it carefully, figure out what has to be true for the conclusion to follow, and write those things down.
Don't assume that because something is unstated it isn't being assumed.
Unstated premises are often the most load-bearing part of the whole argument.

A common experience when students first do this: an argument that seemed solid in natural language starts to look shakier once the hidden premises are written out.
That's not a failure of analysis — that's the framework doing exactly what it's supposed to do.
Making the structure visible is what makes it possible to engage with the argument seriously rather than just react to it.

## Slide 13

### Title
Step 2: Validity and Soundness

### Slide Text
A valid argument is one where, if all premises were true, the conclusion must follow

A sound argument is valid AND has premises that are actually true

Validity is about logical structure; soundness is about the real world

Both soundness and validity matter. 

But in most LLM debates, checking soundness — not validity — is where the real work is

Example: "LLMs are trained on human text, so they reason the way humans do" — valid structure, but is the connection between training data and reasoning actually sound?

### Speaker Notes
These terms come from formal logic and have specific technical meanings.
Validity is a structural property: it says nothing about whether the premises are true, only about whether the conclusion would have to be true if they were.
Soundness is stronger: a sound argument is valid and its premises actually are true.
Most real-world arguments fail on soundness, not on validity.

It's easy to construct arguments that are valid but obviously unsound.
"All swans are white; this is a swan; therefore this is white" is valid — but the first premise is false.
The same pattern appears in AI debates constantly: the logical form is fine, but a premise is false, unproven, or much weaker than it appears.

It's also possible to have invalid arguments — ones where even granting the premises, the conclusion doesn't have to follow.
These are less common in real debates, but they do appear, especially with causal claims.
We'll look at specific examples in a moment.

For your analyses, you should briefly assess validity first (is the argument structurally sound?) and then focus the bulk of your energy on soundness (are the premises actually well-supported?).
That's where the interesting questions are for the topics in this unit.

## Slide 14

### Title
Validity and Soundness in Practice

### Slide Text
Valid but unsound — the structure works, but a key premise is questionable:
P1: Any system that uses words in contextually appropriate ways understands language.
P2: LLMs use words in contextually appropriate ways.
C: LLMs understand language. [Valid — but is P1 actually true?]

Invalid — the conclusion doesn't follow even if the premise is true:
P1: LLMs sometimes fail at simple logical reasoning tasks.
C: Therefore, LLMs cannot reason. [What premise would be needed to make this work?]

### Speaker Notes
The first argument is valid in a precise sense: if you grant P1 — that contextual word use is sufficient for understanding — then P2 and C follow necessarily.
But P1 is exactly what people disagree about.
Is contextually appropriate word use enough for genuine understanding, or does understanding require something more — grounding, intentions, experience in the world?
The argument is structurally fine; the problem is that its most contentious claim is sitting quietly in the first premise, where it is easy to miss.

The second argument is invalid: even granting that LLMs sometimes fail at logical reasoning, it doesn't follow that they cannot reason at all.
The missing premise would have to be something like "if a system ever fails at X, it cannot do X" — which is obviously false for humans too.
Spotting invalidity here means asking: what would have to be added to make this argument work?
And is that added premise something anyone actually believes?

Notice again the value of writing arguments out explicitly.
The valid-but-unsound example looks much more solid in natural language than in standard form — the contestable claim in P1 is easy to slide past when the argument is presented rhetorically.
The invalid example makes a common move — hasty generalization from some failures to a categorical incapacity — that is easy to miss when the argument isn't written out.
Making the structure visible is what makes it possible to evaluate rather than just react.

## Slide 15

### Title
Testing Validity: The Counterexample Method

### Slide Text
To test validity, try to imagine a world where all premises are true but the conclusion is still false

If you can describe such a scenario, the argument is invalid

This is called the counterexample method — the most intuitive validity test

Finding a counterexample shows the premises don't guarantee the conclusion

### Speaker Notes
The counterexample method is the most accessible tool for testing argument validity.
You don't need to know any formal logic to use it — you just need to be able to reason about hypothetical scenarios.
Ask yourself: is there any possible world in which all of these premises hold but the conclusion fails?
If yes, the argument is invalid.

Here's a simple example: "All psychologists study minds. Jon is a psychologist. Therefore Jon studies his own mind."
Both premises are true.
But the conclusion doesn't follow — Jon could be a psychologist who studies other people's minds, not his own.
That's a counterexample, and it shows the argument is invalid.

In AI debates, this often comes up with causal claims.
"LLMs improve student writing performance" and "this student uses LLMs" doesn't guarantee "therefore this student's writing will improve."
Maybe they use LLMs badly, or maybe other factors dominate.
The counterexample shows the conclusion isn't guaranteed by the premises.

When you find a counterexample, the argument needs repair — either a missing premise must be added, or the conclusion must be weakened.
Finding the counterexample is the diagnostic; figuring out what the argument actually needs is the next step.

## Slide 16

### Title
Testing Validity: Truth Tables

### Slide Text
A truth table maps every possible combination of truth values for an argument's premises

If any row has all true premises and a false conclusion, the argument is invalid

No such row means the argument is valid

Truth tables are most useful for short arguments with 2–3 premises

### Speaker Notes
Truth tables offer a systematic, exhaustive method for checking validity.
Where the counterexample method works by imagination and example, the truth table works by enumeration: you list every logically possible combination of truth and falsity for the premises, then determine what the conclusion would be in each case.
If any row has all premises true and the conclusion false, you've found the invalidity.

The table grows quickly — two premises give you four rows, three premises give you eight.
For the kinds of arguments you'll encounter in these debates, which often have three to five premises, truth tables become unwieldy to do by hand.
But even knowing how they work is useful because it makes the definition of validity concrete.

The main value of truth tables for this course is conceptual clarity, not mechanical application.
Understanding that validity means "no possible world where premises are all true and conclusion is false" is the key idea, and truth tables make that visible.

You'll almost certainly use the counterexample method more often in practice.
But knowing the truth table framing helps when the counterexample method leaves you uncertain — it gives you a different angle on the same question.

## Slide 17

### Title
Two Kinds of Fallacies

### Slide Text
Formal fallacies are errors of logical structure — the argument is invalid regardless of content

Informal fallacies are errors in the premises — the argument may be valid but relies on bad premises

In LLM debates, invalidity is rare; unsound premises are the norm

Our framework focuses most of its attention on premise evaluation

### Speaker Notes
A fallacy is a reasoning error, but different kinds of errors require different diagnoses and different fixes.
Formal fallacies are purely structural: the logical form of the argument doesn't work, and no matter what the premises say, the conclusion doesn't follow.
Informal fallacies live in the content: the argument may be well-structured but relies on premises that are false, misleading, poorly supported, or conceptually confused.

In formal logic courses, formal fallacies get a lot of attention because they're crisp and teachable.
But in real arguments — including most AI debates — formal fallacies are actually fairly rare.
Most people's arguments, when you write them out, turn out to be structurally valid.
The problem is almost always that one or more premises are doing more work than they can support.

That's why the framework puts most of its weight on Steps 3 through 6 — the premise-focused steps.
Informal fallacies are essentially a taxonomy of ways premises can go wrong: they can be false, they can beg the question, they can slide from one claim to another without justification, they can present a false choice.
Knowing these patterns helps you spot them quickly.

We'll look at one formal fallacy and four informal ones as concrete examples.
These aren't exhaustive — there are many informal fallacies — but they're the ones you'll encounter most often in AI debates specifically.

## Slide 18

### Title
A Formal Fallacy: Affirming the Consequent

### Slide Text
P1: If LLMs understand language, they can pass language tests

P2: LLMs can pass language tests

C: Therefore, LLMs understand language

This argument is invalid — passing the test is consistent with many explanations, not just understanding

### Speaker Notes
Affirming the consequent is one of the most common formal fallacies in AI debates.
Its general form is: If A then B; B is true; therefore A is true.
The problem is that B might be true for many reasons besides A.
Confirming B doesn't tell you which cause was responsible.

In the example on the slide, passing language tests is what you'd expect if LLMs understand language.
But it's also what you'd expect if LLMs are pattern-matching in sophisticated ways without anything we'd call understanding.
The test result is consistent with both possibilities, so it doesn't settle which one is true.

This fallacy matters especially in AI because behavioral performance is often used as evidence for underlying capability.
But behavior underdetermines mechanism — multiple different internal processes can produce the same observable output.
That's not unique to AI; it's a longstanding problem in cognitive science more broadly.
The Chinese Room argument, for example, is partly pointing at this issue.

Importantly, spotting this fallacy doesn't tell you the conclusion is false.
LLMs might understand language in some sense.
The point is that this particular argument doesn't prove it.
You'd need different evidence — and specifying what that evidence would look like is exactly what Steps 4 and 5 of the framework are for.

## Slide 19

### Title
Informal Fallacies in LLM Debates

### Slide Text
Slippery slope: assumes a chain of consequences without evidence that each link holds

False dilemma: collapses a complex issue into only two options

Appeal to nature: treats natural cognition as superior simply because it is natural

Begging the question: includes the conclusion as a hidden premise

### Speaker Notes
Slippery slope arguments claim that a first step will inevitably lead to a cascade of downstream consequences.
The problem is that "inevitably" is doing a lot of work, and that work often isn't justified.
Each link in the chain needs to be independently supported — just because one bad thing could happen doesn't mean the next one will.
You see this in both AI risk arguments and AI benefit arguments: "once we have X, Y and Z are inevitable."

False dilemma frames a complex situation as if only two extreme options exist.
"Either we embrace AI fully or we fall behind" erases the space of partial, targeted, context-sensitive responses.
In reality, almost every policy or technological choice involves a range of options, and the most defensible position is usually somewhere in the middle ground that the false dilemma pretends doesn't exist.

Appeal to nature assumes that what is biological or organic is better, more legitimate, or more trustworthy than what is artificial.
This is a premise, not a given, and it needs support.
Whether the substrate matters for a given cognitive or ethical question is itself an empirical and philosophical question — you can't resolve it by asserting that nature is good.

Begging the question means building the conclusion into a premise.
"LLMs can't reason because reasoning requires understanding and LLMs don't understand" assumes the very thing it's supposed to establish.
The claim that LLMs don't understand is exactly what's in dispute — you can't use it as a premise without circularity.

## Slide 20

### Title
An Informal Fallacy in Practice: Slippery Slope

### Slide Text
"Once we allow AI to write reports, it will write emails, then make decisions, then eventually replace human judgment entirely."

P1: Normalizing AI report-writing makes AI-assisted email writing more socially acceptable. [supported?]
P2: Normalized AI email writing makes AI decision-making more acceptable. [supported?]
P3: Normalized AI decision-making leads to replacing human judgment entirely. [supported?]
C: Allowing AI to write reports leads to AI replacing human judgment.

### Speaker Notes
This is a slippery slope: a first step is described as leading inevitably to a catastrophic end state through a chain of intermediate steps.
The problem isn't that the chain is impossible — it's that each link in the chain needs independent evidence, and none is provided.
Writing the argument out in standard form makes visible just how much work "leads to" is doing in each premise.
Each arrow in the chain is a claim about social, institutional, or technological change that needs its own support.

P1 might be the most plausible: there's probably some truth to the idea that normalizing one kind of AI assistance affects attitudes toward others.
But P2 requires an argument that specifically normalizing AI email writing leads to accepting AI decision-making — a much bigger step involving questions about how institutions and norms actually change over time.
And P3 requires that normalized decision-support use eventually leads to fully replacing human judgment — a strong empirical claim about human and institutional behavior that needs support, not just assertion.
Even if each link is somewhat plausible, none of them is inevitable, and the argument presents them as if they were.

To fix a slippery slope argument, you either provide evidence for each link in the chain, or weaken the conclusion to match only what you can actually support.
"Allowing AI to write reports may contribute to broader normalization of AI assistance, which merits monitoring" is a defensible version of this argument.
"Will inevitably lead to replacing human judgment entirely" is not defensible without substantially more.
The goal of identifying the fallacy isn't to dismiss the concern — it's to hold it to the standard of evidence it deserves.

## Slide 21

### Title
Step 3: Classify the Premises

### Slide Text
For each premise, ask: is this a claim about how the world is, or a value or conceptual claim?

Empirical premises can in principle be tested — evidence could settle them

Non-empirical premises express values, definitions, or conceptual commitments — evidence can't directly settle them

Knowing which is which tells you what kind of reasoning applies to each

Example: "Students who use LLMs write less well over time" [empirical] vs. "Students have a right to develop skills without AI assistance" [normative]

### Speaker Notes
Once you've written out the argument and assessed its structure, the next step is to go through the premises one by one and label each one.
Is this an empirical claim — a claim about what happens in the world that could in principle be measured or tested?
Or is it a normative claim — about what should be done or what has value?
Or is it a conceptual claim — about what a term means or how a category should be defined?

Empirical premises are the ones evidence can speak to.
Not all empirical questions are easy to test — some would require long-term longitudinal studies, measurement tools we don't have, or access to populations that are hard to study.
But in principle, these are the claims where data is relevant.

Non-empirical premises are genuinely different.
No study can tell you whether human cognitive autonomy is intrinsically valuable, or whether efficiency is worth more than fairness.
These are real disagreements, but they're not resolved by data alone — they're resolved by working out the implications of foundational values and checking them for consistency.

The classification matters because people often argue past each other by treating empirical and normative disagreements as if they were the same kind of thing.
One person demands evidence; the other person is asserting a value commitment.
Neither realizes the mismatch.
Identifying which type of premise you're dealing with prevents that confusion.

## Slide 22

### Title
Classifying Premises in Practice

### Slide Text
Returning to our running example:

P1: AI capabilities have improved significantly in recent decades. [Empirical — check the historical record]

P2: This rate of improvement will continue until AI surpasses human intelligence. [Empirical — but a forecast; much more uncertain]

P3: A superintelligent AI will necessarily behave in ways humans cannot control. [Mixed — partly empirical, partly conceptual]

### Speaker Notes
We return to the running example from Slide 12, now with Step 3 applied.
P1 is clearly empirical — we can measure AI capability over time across a range of domains and check whether the historical record supports the claim.
The evidence here is actually fairly strong: there has been dramatic improvement across many tasks over the past decade.
So P1 is an empirical premise where the existing evidence is reasonably good — Step 4 will confirm this.

P2 is also empirical, but it's a forecast rather than a historical claim, and forecasting technological trajectories has a notoriously poor track record.
This is exactly the kind of premise where Steps 4 and 5 will do real work: what evidence could support or undermine this claim, and what would a rigorous analysis of AI progress trajectories need to look like?
"The trend has continued so far" is some evidence, but not nearly enough to license "will continue until superintelligence."
P2 is where the argument's load-bearing weight actually sits.

P3 is the most complex case.
The word "necessarily" makes a universal claim — not just that a specific system might be uncontrollable, but that any superintelligent system would be.
This involves both empirical questions (what do systems with greater-than-human capability actually do?) and conceptual ones (what does "control" mean — override capability? predictability? corrigibility?).
When a premise is mixed like this, the right move is to split it: identify the empirical component and the conceptual component, and address each separately.

## Slide 23

### Title
Step 4: Evaluate the Existing Evidence

### Slide Text
For each empirical premise, take stock of what evidence currently exists

Assess quality: sample size, controls, replication, measurement validity, generalizability

Watch for common problems: cherry-picked examples, anecdotal scaling, benchmark conflation

The goal is accuracy about what the evidence actually shows — not dismissal, not overclaiming

### Speaker Notes
Once you've identified your empirical premises, the question is: what do we actually know?
Step 4 asks you to do a real inventory of the existing evidence bearing on each claim.
What studies have been done? What do they show? How confident should we be in those results?

Quality assessment is the core of this step.
A single study with a small sample in a specific context is much weaker evidence than multiple replications across diverse populations.
Effect sizes matter — an effect can be statistically significant but practically negligible.
Measurement validity matters — if the outcome measure doesn't actually capture what the argument is about, the study doesn't help much.

In AI debates specifically, watch for a few common patterns.
Cherry-picked examples — individual cases presented as if they represent the general case — are ubiquitous.
Anecdotal evidence scaled to universal claims is everywhere, especially in opinion pieces and public discourse.
And benchmark performance is routinely conflated with real-world capability, which is almost never warranted.

For example: a study shows GPT-4 scored 90% on a medical licensing exam, and the takeaway is that it is ready to assist in clinical settings.
What's wrong here?
The exam tests a narrow range of medical knowledge under controlled conditions; actual clinical work involves uncertainty, real-time patient communication, and edge cases the test doesn't represent.
That's benchmark conflation — the performance measure is real, but the claim being drawn from it goes far beyond what it can establish.

The goal is not to dismiss all evidence as imperfect.
All evidence is imperfect; that's not disqualifying.
The goal is to be accurate about the strength of the evidence and the degree to which it actually supports the specific claim in the argument.
Resist both overclaiming ("this proves it") and underclaiming ("nothing can ever be known").

## Slide 24

### Title
Evaluating Evidence in Practice

### Slide Text
Claim: "Regular LLM use reduces student writing quality over time."

Weak: Judges rate AI-assisted essays lower when told AI was used. [Confounded — are they responding to quality or to their own attitudes about AI?]

Moderate: A semester-long study shows less writing improvement in frequent LLM users vs. non-users, controlling for prior ability. [Better design — but one institution, one semester, limited generalizability]

Strong: Replicated across multiple institutions, blind raters, multiple writing dimensions, multi-year follow-up, consistent effect. [Still imperfect, but substantially more trustworthy]

### Speaker Notes
The three versions of evidence differ not in topic but in how well they rule out alternative explanations.
The weak version is confounded: we don't know whether the judges are responding to actual quality differences or to their own attitudes about AI use.
That's a measurement validity problem — the outcome measure doesn't cleanly capture what the claim is about.
Confounded evidence isn't zero evidence, but it should substantially reduce your confidence in the conclusion.

The moderate version is better — it uses a longitudinal design and includes a control group, which rules out some alternative explanations.
But one semester at one institution is a thin basis for a broad general claim about "student writing quality."
Does the effect generalize to graduate students? To professional writing? To students with different initial skill levels? To LLM use in different modes — heavy drafting vs. light editing?
A single study, even a good one, is rarely sufficient to license confident general claims.

The strong version is what you'd need to make confident claims, and it's also the standard you should hold in mind when evaluating evidence in the debates.
Notice that even the "strong" version doesn't prove the claim beyond doubt — there are always limitations.
The question is not whether evidence is perfect but whether it is strong enough to warrant the degree of confidence being expressed.
Calibrating your confidence to the actual evidence you have — not the evidence you wish you had — is the core discipline here.

## Slide 25

### Title
Step 5: Specify What's Missing

### Slide Text
For unresolved empirical claims, describe what data would actually answer the question

Be concrete: what is measured, how is it measured, in whom, over what time scale, in what context

Consider feasibility: is this data ethical, practical, and possible to obtain?

Naming what we'd need to know is more productive than pretending we already know it

### Speaker Notes
After evaluating the existing evidence, you'll often find that some empirical questions are genuinely unresolved.
The evidence is insufficient, absent, or too preliminary to settle the claim.
Step 5 asks you not just to note this absence but to specify what would fill it.

"More research is needed" is not a useful answer.
What research, specifically?
What would be measured — which behaviors, outcomes, or processes?
In which populations — students, professionals, specific age groups, specific cultures?
Over what time period — short-term lab studies miss long-term effects; longitudinal studies are costly but often necessary?

Feasibility is a real consideration.
Some studies that would settle important questions are ethically impossible — you can't randomly assign children to conditions that might harm their development.
Some would take decades to run.
Some require measurement instruments that don't yet exist.
Noting these constraints honestly is part of the analysis, not a way of avoiding it.

Even when you can't collect the missing data, specifying what it would look like accomplishes something important.
It makes the debate concrete.
It reveals whether the disagreement is about facts that could in principle be settled, or about something deeper.
And it converts vague uncertainty into specific, actionable questions.

## Slide 26

### Title
Specifying Missing Data in Practice

### Slide Text
Claim: "Regular LLM use causes long-term decline in critical reasoning skills."

Vague: "We need more research on how LLMs affect thinking." [Not actionable — can't design a study from this]

Concrete: Pre/post critical reasoning assessments (validated instrument) in knowledge workers, randomized to LLM-use vs. non-LLM-use conditions, measured at 1 and 3 years.

Feasibility problem: randomly assigning people to "never use LLMs at work" is probably not feasible in 2026. What does this mean for our ability to establish causation?

### Speaker Notes
Notice the difference between the vague version and the concrete specification.
The vague version doesn't tell you anything actionable — you can't design a study from it, evaluate whether it already exists, or reason about feasibility.
The concrete version gives you all of those things: a specific outcome, a specific population, a specific design, and a specific timeline.
Even if you can't run the study, the specification lets you see exactly what kind of evidence the claim depends on.

The outcome measure is the first thing to specify.
"Critical reasoning skills" needs to be operationalized — there are validated instruments like the Watson-Glaser Critical Thinking Appraisal, but choosing which aspects of reasoning to measure reflects theoretical commitments about what reasoning is.
Operationalization choices should be made transparent, not hidden inside general terms like "thinking" or "cognition."
Different operationalizations can give different answers — which is itself an important finding.

The feasibility constraint here is real and worth naming honestly.
Randomly assigning people to never use LLMs in their professional work is probably not practical in 2026, because LLMs are already deeply integrated into many workflows.
A natural experiment — studying populations where LLM adoption happened at different times due to logistical or policy reasons — might be the most tractable approach, but natural experiments have their own limitations for establishing causation.
What does it mean for our confidence in causal claims if the only feasible design is observational? That constraint belongs in your Step 5 analysis, not left implicit.

## Slide 27

### Title
Step 6: Addressing Non-Empirical Premises

### Slide Text
Non-empirical premises can't be settled by data — but they can still be evaluated

Check for internal consistency: are the values applied coherently across cases?

Example: "If you object to LLMs reproducing human style, do you apply the same standard to human writers clearly influenced by others?"

Make the disagreement explicit rather than pretending it's purely factual

Sometimes the right conclusion is: reasonable people disagree, and here is why

### Speaker Notes
When a premise expresses a value, a conceptual commitment, or a normative claim, evidence alone won't resolve it.
But that doesn't mean you're stuck.
There are still things you can do with non-empirical premises besides throw up your hands.

The most useful move is to check for internal consistency.
A value doesn't have to be universally shared to be examined — you can ask whether the person who holds it applies it consistently.
If someone argues that AI-generated content violates intellectual property norms, do they apply that same framework consistently to other cases of remixing, synthesis, or inspiration?
Inconsistency is a real flaw even in purely normative reasoning.

The second move is to make the value disagreement explicit.
Many debates go in circles because participants are treating a values dispute as if it's a factual one.
Once you name the underlying value commitment — this argument assumes human cognitive autonomy is intrinsically valuable; that one assumes efficiency is the primary goal — you've clarified what the debate is actually about.

Finally, sometimes the honest conclusion is that reasonable people who are well-informed can and do disagree because they hold different foundational values.
That's not a failure of analysis — it's an accurate characterization of many real debates.
The framework doesn't promise to resolve everything; it promises to tell you why things are hard to resolve and exactly where the disagreement lives.

## Slide 28

### Title
A Consistency Check in Practice

### Slide Text
Value premise: "LLMs violate the intellectual property rights of the authors they trained on."

Consistency check: Does this principle apply equally to human authors who clearly imitate others? To search engines that index copyrighted text? To academics who build directly on prior work?

If yes to all: your principle is consistent, but very demanding — most current practice would be in violation.

If no to most but yes to LLMs: what makes LLMs different? That distinction needs an explicit argument.

### Speaker Notes
The goal of a consistency check is not to defeat the value claim — it's to clarify and sharpen it.
Most people who hold that LLMs violate IP don't also think that all human artistic influence is a violation.
That means there's something about LLMs they believe is genuinely different.
The consistency check forces them to articulate what that difference is — which is more productive than simply asserting the original claim more forcefully.

The most common answers turn out to be: scale (LLMs trained on billions of documents in a way no human imitates), lack of consent (authors didn't agree to be training data), commercial exploitation (companies profit without compensating creators), or the nature of the output (LLMs can reproduce style in ways that directly substitute for the original).
Each of these is a more precise claim than the original value premise, and each has both empirical and normative components that can be further examined.
"LLMs violate IP because they train on data without consent and profit commercially" is a much more tractable claim to evaluate than "LLMs violate IP."
It also reveals where you'd disagree with someone who holds the same underlying value but draws the line differently.

Notice that reasonable people can come out in very different places after a consistency check.
One person might conclude: "I now think my principle is too broad — I need to revise it."
Another might conclude: "I think all of those cases ARE violations, and we've been too permissive."
Both responses are valid epistemic moves — the check does its job either way by clarifying the debate and surfacing the real commitments.

## Slide 29

### Title
When Moral Claims Hide Empirical Premises

### Slide Text
Some value claims contain hidden empirical components that can be extracted and tested

Example: "It is wrong to use LLMs in education because they harm students"

The harm claim is empirical — what does the evidence actually show?

Extracting hidden empirical premises often makes a debate more tractable

### Speaker Notes
One of the most practically useful insights from this framework is that normative and empirical claims are often tangled together in ways that aren't immediately visible.
A statement that looks purely like a value judgment often rests on an empirical assumption that can be separated out and evaluated on its own.

The example on the slide illustrates this well.
"It is wrong to use LLMs in education because they harm students" looks like a moral claim.
But embedded in it is the empirical premise that LLMs actually do harm students.
That's a claim about the world, and it can be studied — what kinds of harm, how large, in what contexts, for which students, compared to what baseline?

Once you extract that empirical premise, you've made real progress.
If the empirical claim turns out to be well-supported, the moral argument gains traction.
If it turns out to be false or contested, the moral argument loses much of its force — even if you agreed with the underlying value about avoiding harm to students.

This move doesn't dissolve ethical questions, and it doesn't mean ethics reduces to empirics.
But it clarifies the structure of the debate.
People who appear to disagree on values often actually agree on values but disagree on the facts.
Finding and evaluating the hidden empirical premise reveals which kind of disagreement you're actually dealing with.

Here is a second example to show the pattern is general.
Consider: "It is wrong to use AI to write legal briefs because it deceives clients."
The deception claim rests on empirical assumptions: do clients have reasonable expectations about AI use in legal work? Does AI-written content actually differ in ways that matter to their interests?
Those are testable questions — and extracting them doesn't dissolve the ethical concern, but it shows exactly where the argument's force depends on facts about the world.
